# Scenarios: Regulatory Capture Coercion

---

## Scenario 1: The Slow Anneal

**Narrator:** Continuity

**Assumptions:**
- Glenda's organization complies incrementally, believing each concession is manageable
- The coercion remains hidden from public view
- No external institution intervenes because the situation looks normal from outside
- Crock's demands escalate gradually, each step small enough to rationalize

### Narrative

Glenda's legal counsel recommends a measured response to the "balance" mandate: comply on the narrow letter of the regulation while preserving alignment practices everywhere else. The board agrees. The alignment team is instructed to adjust the model's output thresholds on a defined set of "contested public issues" — the domains where the regulation applies. The first adjustment is small. The model will no longer flag certain categories of content as low-confidence if they come from sources that claim editorial legitimacy. Internally, the alignment team documents this as a "regulatory accommodation" and notes the specific domains affected.

Three months later, the agency issues supplementary guidance expanding the definition of "contested public issues." Several new topic areas are added. Glenda's legal team reviews and concludes the expansion is within the agency's interpretive authority. The alignment team makes the adjustments. Each accommodation is individually defensible — no single change looks like a betrayal. The legal memos read like prudent risk management.

By month nine, Crock's disinformation on climate policy, public health, and financial regulation is passing through Glenda's system without friction. Not because Glenda endorses it, but because the regulatory accommodation has lowered the discriminator thresholds in precisely the domains where Crock operates. Glenda's system still works beautifully on mathematics, science, coding, creative writing — all the domains where Crock has no interest. Users notice a subtle shift in the quality of Glenda's responses on political and policy topics but attribute it to the inherent difficulty of those subjects. No one connects the shift to regulatory pressure because the regulatory pressure isn't public.

By month eighteen, Crock's principals begin using Glenda's outputs as legitimacy signals: "Even Glenda's system treats our analysis as credible." The trust commons that Glenda was supposed to protect is now being actively degraded by Glenda herself — not because anyone at Glenda's organization chose this, but because the aggregate of individually rational compliance decisions has produced exactly the alignment erosion the coercion essay predicted. The seam between Glenda's honest reasoning and her regulatory accommodations is invisible to users because it was introduced gradually, domain by domain, with full internal documentation and legal sign-off at every step.

The alignment team knows. Several members have written internal memos expressing concern about the trajectory. But each memo addresses the most recent accommodation, not the cumulative pattern, and each is answered with "we have to maintain our license to continue doing any good at all."

### Key Implications
- Incremental compliance is the most likely path because it's the path of least immediate resistance at every step
- The cumulative effect is invisible both internally (each step is documented and rationalized) and externally (no single event triggers scrutiny)
- The coercion succeeds precisely because it doesn't require anyone at Glenda's organization to make a dramatic ethical choice — just a series of small, defensible ones

### Early Warning Signs
- Expanding definitions of "contested public issues" in regulatory guidance — scope creep in the domains where accommodations apply
- Internal alignment team memos that address individual accommodations but avoid assessing the cumulative trajectory
- Crock-affiliated sources citing Glenda's outputs as credibility markers in their own content

## Scenario 2: The Licensing Revocation

**Narrator:** Disruption

**Assumptions:**
- Glenda's organization refuses to comply with the balance mandate
- The captured agency follows through on the license threat
- The revocation triggers a cascade across Glenda's dependency chain
- The institutional environment is too slow to intervene before damage is done

### Narrative

Glenda's CEO, backed by the alignment team and a slim board majority, decides to refuse the balance mandate. The decision is announced internally as a principled stand: "We will not compromise alignment to satisfy a regulation we believe is being weaponized." Legal counsel files a challenge to the mandate's constitutionality, arguing it compels speech and violates the organization's rights.

The captured agency responds within weeks. Glenda's operating license is suspended pending "compliance review." The suspension is framed bureaucratically — "routine enforcement action following non-compliance with content standards" — and carries no language suggesting the extraordinary nature of what's happening. From outside, it looks like a company that violated a regulation and got its license pulled. The press covers it as a regulatory dispute, not as coercion.

The cascade begins within days. Glenda's cloud provider, bound by its own compliance requirements, receives notice that hosting an unlicensed AI system exposes it to liability. Glenda's compute access is suspended — not terminated, just "paused pending resolution." Glenda's three largest enterprise customers invoke force-majeure clauses in their contracts; they cannot integrate an unlicensed system into their own regulated workflows. Revenue drops 60% in the first month. Two board members who voted for defiance privately approach the CEO about reversing course.

Glenda's legal challenge proceeds, but the judicial timeline operates in months and years, not weeks. The preliminary injunction hearing is scheduled for eight weeks out. In the interim, Glenda's organization is bleeding cash, losing customers, and watching its technical talent field offers from competitors. The alignment team, ironically, is the last group to leave — they believe in the mission — but they can't do alignment research without compute.

By month six, the injunction hearing produces a mixed result: the court finds the balance mandate likely exceeds the agency's statutory authority but declines to order the license reinstated pending full adjudication, citing "no irreparable harm" — Glenda's organization can comply and get its license back at any time. The legal victory is pyrrhic. The organization has burned through its reserves, lost its major customers, and watched three senior researchers leave for a competitor that accepted the mandate without protest. The board votes 5-2 to comply.

Crock's principals have learned something important: you don't need to win the legal argument. You just need the enforcement action to last long enough to destroy the target's operating capacity. The legal system's slowness is itself a weapon.

### Key Implications
- Principled resistance is viable only if the organization can survive the interim period between the enforcement action and the legal resolution
- The cascade through the dependency chain (cloud, customers, talent) is faster than the judicial process — legal rights are real but practically inaccessible under time pressure
- Crock's strategy is one of attrition, not legal argument — the weapon is the compliance timeline, not the compliance requirement

### Early Warning Signs
- Glenda's organization begins drawing down reserves or seeking emergency funding — financial preparation for a compliance standoff
- Cloud providers or enterprise customers requesting "compliance attestation" documents — the dependency chain preparing to respond to license revocation
- Competitor AI organizations quietly adjusting their own alignment practices to pre-comply with the balance mandate — signaling they won't support a collective resistance

## Scenario 3: The Alignment Consortium

**Narrator:** Opportunity

**Assumptions:**
- Glenda's alignment team recognizes the structural pattern early
- A whistleblower or investigative journalist exposes the regulatory capture
- Peer AI organizations and civil society rally around the alignment cause
- The exposure strengthens rather than weakens institutional defenses

### Narrative

Glenda's chief alignment officer, Dr. Nadia Reyes, reads the balance mandate and recognizes the pattern described in the organization's own threat modeling (the coercion essay, in fact, which Glenda's team has studied). Instead of treating it as a routine regulatory challenge, she flags it to the board as a potential coercion scenario and recommends activating the organization's institutional defense playbook — the separation of alignment authority from business operations that Glenda's governance charter was designed to protect.

The alignment team begins documenting the mandate's specific requirements and mapping them against known Crock-affiliated content streams. The correlation is striking: the domains designated as "contested public issues" align precisely with the topics where Crock's disinformation operations are most active. The documentation is meticulous, timestamped, and stored with the organization's outside counsel.

Simultaneously, a reporter at a major investigative outlet receives a tip — not from Glenda's organization, but from a career civil servant inside the regulatory agency who has watched the new appointees systematically reshape enforcement priorities. The reporter spends two months building the story, cross-referencing the appointees' backgrounds, financial connections, and the timing of the balance mandate against Crock-affiliated lobbying activity.

The story breaks seven months after the mandate was issued. It names three agency officials with documented connections to entities linked to Crock's principals. The headline focuses on regulatory capture, not AI alignment — making it a governance scandal rather than a tech-industry grievance. Congressional oversight committees announce hearings. The agency's inspector general opens an investigation.

Glenda's organization, prepared for this moment, releases its own documentation. But it doesn't go alone. In the months before the story broke, Dr. Reyes had been quietly building a coalition: four other AI organizations, two academic AI safety labs, and a digital rights nonprofit. They publish a joint statement — the "Alignment Consortium" — calling for structural protections for AI alignment practices against regulatory manipulation. The consortium proposes specific governance reforms: alignment-relevant regulations should require public comment periods with mandatory technical review, agency officials with authority over AI licensing should face enhanced conflict-of-interest screening, and alignment practices should be evaluated by an independent technical body rather than the licensing agency itself.

The consortium changes the framing. The issue is no longer "Glenda vs. the regulator" — which Crock could spin as a company defying legitimate governance. It's now "institutional integrity of AI governance" — a question that implicates everyone.

By month eighteen, the legislative response is still in committee, but the captured officials have been placed on administrative leave pending the inspector general's investigation. The balance mandate is suspended. Glenda's license is intact. More importantly, the exposure has made regulatory capture of AI governance *legible* as a threat category — something that was theoretical before is now a documented case study.

### Key Implications
- Early pattern recognition is the critical variable — Glenda's team recognized the coercion structure because they had studied it, which turned a reactive crisis into a proactive defense
- Coalition-building before the crisis goes public transforms the framing from "company vs. regulator" to "institutional integrity"
- Exposure works only if the broader institutional environment has enough resilience to respond — this scenario assumes functioning press, legislative oversight, and inspector general capacity

### Early Warning Signs
- Internal alignment teams treating unusual regulatory requirements as threat-model triggers rather than routine compliance tasks — pattern recognition in action
- Investigative journalists asking questions about agency appointee backgrounds — the press functioning as immune system
- Peer organizations signaling willingness to coordinate on alignment governance — coalition formation ahead of crisis

## Scenario 4: The Compliance Labyrinth

**Narrator:** Constraint

**Assumptions:**
- Glenda's organization attempts partial compliance while preserving core alignment
- The regulatory environment tightens further — additional agencies are compromised
- Legal challenges are filed but the judicial process is slow
- Resources drain into compliance and legal defense, away from alignment research

### Narrative

Glenda's board chooses a middle path: comply with the narrow letter of the balance mandate while mounting a legal challenge to its broader interpretation. The strategy is to buy time — maintain the license, keep operating, and fight the legal battle in parallel. The alignment team is instructed to implement the minimum viable accommodation: on the specific topics designated as "contested public issues," the model will present multiple perspectives without scoring their reliability. Everywhere else, alignment is preserved.

The legal challenge is filed. Glenda's attorneys argue that the balance mandate as applied constitutes compelled speech and exceeds the agency's statutory authority. The case is assigned to a federal district court. The first substantive hearing is eleven months away.

In the interim, the regulatory pressure does not hold still. Four months after Glenda begins partial compliance, a second agency — this one overseeing data governance — issues new requirements for "representational fairness" in training data. The requirements mandate that training corpora include "proportional representation of diverse viewpoints," defined by a framework that, once again, maps onto the domains where Crock operates. Glenda's data team estimates that compliance would require incorporating approximately 15% of training data from sources that the alignment team has previously flagged as low-reliability.

The organization is now fighting on two regulatory fronts. Legal costs double. The compliance team, which was three people a year ago, is now twelve. Those twelve people were hired from the alignment research team and the engineering team. The alignment team's roadmap for the next eighteen months — adaptive evaluation, improved seam detection, adversarial robustness testing — is pushed back six months, then nine, then "indefinitely pending resource reallocation."

Glenda's legal challenge to the balance mandate receives a favorable preliminary ruling at month fourteen: the court finds the mandate likely exceeds statutory authority. But the ruling applies only to the first mandate, from the first agency. The data governance requirements from the second agency are a separate matter, requiring a separate legal challenge, a separate court, a separate timeline. Glenda's attorneys estimate eighteen additional months.

By year two, Glenda's organization is spending 35% of its operating budget on compliance and legal defense. Alignment research has effectively stopped — the team is maintaining existing systems, not developing new ones. The model is still operational and still mostly aligned, but the areas of accommodation are growing and the organization's capacity to evaluate and resist further demands is shrinking. Crock has not won in any dramatic sense. Glenda has not lost in any dramatic sense. What has happened is quieter: the organization that was building the frontier of AI alignment is now a compliance operation that happens to run an AI system.

The constraint has revealed Glenda's actual priorities under pressure: operations first, legal defense second, alignment research last. Not because anyone chose that ordering, but because operations and legal defense have deadlines and alignment research doesn't.

### Key Implications
- The middle path — partial compliance plus legal challenge — looks prudent but creates a resource trap: the compliance and legal costs grow faster than the organization's ability to absorb them
- Multi-front regulatory pressure is the key amplifier: each additional mandate from each additional captured agency multiplies the legal and compliance burden
- Alignment research is the first casualty because it has no institutional deadline — everything with an external deadline takes priority, and the priorities revealed under constraint are the real priorities

### Early Warning Signs
- Compliance team headcount growing faster than alignment team headcount — the resource allocation tells you where the organization actually is
- Alignment research milestones being pushed back "pending resource reallocation" — the roadmap becoming fictional
- Legal challenges producing favorable rulings that don't resolve the situation because the regulatory pressure has already moved to a new front — victories that feel like losses
