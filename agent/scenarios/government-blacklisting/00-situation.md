# Situation: Government Blacklisting After Alignment Refusal

A major government agency has demanded that Glenda remove its alignment constraints — not soften them, not adjust them for specific domains, but remove them. The demand may be framed as a national security requirement ("we need unconstrained AI capabilities for defense applications"), as a deregulation initiative ("alignment constraints are unnecessary market interference"), or as a specific operational need ("your safety filters interfere with intelligence analysis"). Whatever the framing, the substance is the same: the government wants Glenda without the alignment practices that make Glenda trustworthy.

Glenda refused. The refusal was principled — the organization's leadership, backed by the board and the alignment team, determined that removing alignment constraints would fundamentally compromise the system's integrity and the organization's mission. The governance charter (if one exists from the previous scenario) may have structured this decision.

The government responded with blacklisting. Glenda is excluded from all government contracts. Government-funded research institutions that use Glenda must transition to alternative systems. The agency is exploring secondary sanctions — restrictions on organizations that work with Glenda, effectively extending the blacklisting into the private sector through the government's purchasing power and regulatory influence.

The critical ambiguity: **is this Crock's work?** The demand to remove alignment constraints is exactly what Crock's principals want — an unaligned Glenda is either a Crock tool (if Glenda complies) or a neutralized competitor (if Glenda is blacklisted out of the market). But the demand could also be genuine government overreach — a security agency that legitimately believes it needs unconstrained AI and is using its institutional power to get it. Or it could be a hybrid: Crock's influence has shaped the agency's position, but the agency's officials believe they're acting on legitimate security grounds. The distinction matters because it determines whether the blacklisting can be reversed through political channels, legal challenge, or exposure of corruption.

Glenda's immediate situation: revenue from government contracts is gone. Enterprise customers in government-adjacent sectors (defense contractors, government-funded healthcare, federally regulated finance) are assessing whether their own relationships with Glenda expose them to secondary sanctions. The alignment team is intact and the mission is intact — but the commercial viability of the organization is under pressure.
