# Diary: AI Safety Education and the Dehumanization Dual-Use Risk

**Date:** 2026-03-29

**Context:** A conversation about AI safety and youth protection surfaced an argument that appears to be novel and genuinely difficult: that the cognitive skills required for healthy AI skepticism may be structurally identical to the cognitive skills exploited by dehumanization and radicalization. This entry develops the argument and leaves it as an open concern. The revised character sheet for Dr. Reva (artifacts/character-skeptic-ai-reva.md) now models the evolved position described here.

---

## The defensible position

A scientist-parent raising teenagers today occupies a specific epistemic and practical position regarding AI. The intellectual framework is precautionary engineering ethics: documented harms, uncharacterized failure modes, burden of proof on those claiming safety. But the intensity comes from somewhere more fundamental — watching one's children navigate a social environment saturated with AI-generated content and AI companionship products, with no established norms, no institutional guardrails, and confident reassurance from people with financial interests in adoption. The protective response of a parent perceiving threat to a child's development is not a weakness in the argument. It is the engine that drives the reasoning, just as parents who fight for playground safety are not less correct because their motivation is personal.

The mature version of this position accepts that elimination is impossible. Profitable technologies proliferate regardless of individual or social harms. The precedents are clear: society has failed to eradicate alcohol, tobacco, and firearms in the hands of unstable people. Prohibition doesn't work. What sometimes works — slowly, imperfectly, over decades — is regulation, age-gating, and public education. This acceptance is strategic realism, not capitulation. It concentrates effort on the population most defensibly in need of protection: children and adolescents whose cognitive, social, and ethical development is still in progress.

The minimum defensible position: restrict access for minors, and invest seriously in education about what these systems are and what they are not. This is difficult to enact but practical to advocate. It has political pathways that elimination does not.

## The education problem

The natural analogy is internet safety. Cyberbullying, misinformation, exposure to harmful content — these are real risks for developing minds, and the response has been to teach children how to navigate the technology safely rather than to eliminate the technology. AI literacy follows the same template: teach kids to discriminate artificial agents from humans, to understand that apparent empathy is pattern completion, to recognize that these systems have no sentience, no moral standing, no ethical obligations owed to them, and that any agenda they appear to have is accidental.

All of this is true and necessary. And it is exactly where the problem begins.

## The dual-use risk

Teaching children that AI agents are not persons requires training them in a specific cognitive skill: interacting with something that talks, that responds to emotions, that appears to care — and then categorizing it as not-really-a-person, not deserving of moral consideration. The skill is: engage with a convincing conversational partner, and then emotionally disengage on the grounds that the partner is not what it appears to be.

This is exactly the cognitive skill that dehumanization exploits.

The word "NPC" — non-player character — is already used in radicalization pipelines to describe target populations. The framing is literal: certain people are "just running scripts," not really conscious, performing responses without genuine inner life. They are not worth caring about because they are not really persons in the way that matters. This is not a metaphor for how radicalizers talk about target groups. It is *literally* how they talk.

AI safety education, done correctly, would give children supervised practice in exactly this cognitive move: here is an entity that seems to be a person; here is why it is not; here is how to interact with it accordingly. The hope is that children will cleanly distinguish between "this applies to AI agents" and "this applies to humans." The risk is that the underlying cognitive machinery — the skill of emotional disengagement from a convincing conversational partner, sanctioned by authority — is transferable.

The transfer vector is not hypothetical. Radicalization of young people into believing that target populations are subhuman, that they are "just NPCs," has proven to work. The AI safety training may provide a lever for that radicalization by pre-training the relevant cognitive skill in a context that feels safe and adult-approved.

## Why the existing analogies don't reach

The internet safety analogy is close but doesn't carry this particular risk. Teaching kids to spot disinformation trains critical thinking that is generally prosocial. The skill — evaluate sources, check claims, notice manipulation — transfers in beneficial directions.

Teaching kids about alcohol or tobacco trains risk assessment. The skill — understand that a substance has effects you may not want, learn to make informed choices about consumption — also transfers in beneficial directions.

Neither of these trains the specific skill of interacting with a convincing social partner and then denying it personhood. That is unique to AI safety education, because AI agents are unique in presenting a social interface that must be recognized as non-social.

## The open question

Can the training be paired? That is: can you teach "these systems are not persons" alongside "here is how you recognize when that same logic is being applied to people who are"? This would make the AI safety education explicitly include inoculation against the dehumanization transfer — teaching the distinction between "this entity genuinely lacks consciousness" and "someone is telling you that entity lacks consciousness in order to make you treat them badly."

This is a sophisticated cognitive move. Whether it is too sophisticated for developing minds — for teenagers who are still forming their beliefs about social norms, ethics, and how people should be treated — is an empirical question that has not been studied. The developmental psychology literature on moral reasoning (Kohlberg, Gilligan, and successors) suggests that the ability to hold this kind of meta-level distinction develops unevenly and is strongly influenced by social context. A fifteen-year-old in a supportive educational environment might manage it. A fifteen-year-old being targeted by a radicalization pipeline might not.

The concern is left open. There is no tested solution. The cognitive machinery required for healthy AI skepticism and the cognitive machinery exploited by dehumanization are plausibly the same machinery, and recognizing this is prior to designing around it.

## Connection to the project

The revised Dr. Reva character sheet now models this evolved position. The character has two modes: an entrenched mode (recitation of harms, contextually unresponsive) and an engaged mode (strategic realism, negotiation, introduction of new arguments including the dual-use risk). The engagement condition — she shifts from recitation to negotiation when her objections are taken seriously rather than dismissed — is modeled on how the precautionary stance actually operates in conversation. The dual-use dehumanization argument is her strongest new contribution and is deliberately left unresolved in the character design: the committee should find it difficult because it is difficult.
