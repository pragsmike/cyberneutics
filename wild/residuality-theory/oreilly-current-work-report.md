# External research on residuality theory (2026-04-26)

## 1. PhD thesis

I found no publicly indexed thesis record for Barry M. O’Reilly in the sources searched, but I did find strong evidence that he has been presenting himself as a PhD student/researcher and that a thesis was expected rather than already available. A 2022 YouTube session description identifies him as “PhD Student in Complexity Science & …,” and a 2024 transcript says, “My name is Barry O’Reilly. I’m a software architect and researcher, and I’m based in Stockholm in Sweden. I work primarily with research within software …”. [youtube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8Vr_7S45rRU)

The clearest public clue is a June 2024 statement in the Leanpub book page for *Residues: Time, Change, and Uncertainty in Software Architecture* that says, “This work will culminate shortly in the form of a PhD thesis,” but that is still only a publisher-page claim, not a thesis record. I did not find an institutional repository entry, thesis DOI, supervisor name, defence date, abstract, or open-access PDF in the searched sources. The thesis may therefore have been unpublished, not yet defended, or not indexed publicly in the repositories searched. ## 2. Reviews of *Residues* (2024)

Goodreads has the most visible cluster of public reviews I found. One review by Travis (dated 2025-02-13) is negative/mixed: “Interesting, but it feels half baked at best. My takeaway was that it’s a combo of threat modeling with architecture skipping some of the formal design. Which I like! But the text just didn’t quite work for me.”. Another review by Nicola (2025-01-03) is mixed/critical: it says the ideas are “very abstract and not rigorous,” and objects to passages that “reference software developers as some sort of immature people”. A positive Goodreads review by Christian Marques (2024-12-18) praises the book as “excellent,” “succinctly covering the theory,” and “easy to understand,” while still noting it is inspired by “pretty complex concepts”. Alejandro’s review (2024-12-20) is strongly positive, calling it a “profound gap” bridger between philosophy, complexity science, and software architecture. [goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/219197668-residues)

Eric Normand’s Substack essay is a sympathetic external reading rather than a traditional review. He calls residuality “a framework for systems architecture” and says there are “a lot of good ideas in it,” while also criticizing the name: “It’s a terrible name”. Normand’s essay specifically highlights O’Reilly’s critique of current architecture practice, his use of mental simulation of stressors, and his incidence-matrix method. I did not find comparable reviews in the searched web results from InfoQ, IEEE Software, CACM, or major architecture blogs within the time budget. [ericnormand.substack](https://ericnormand.substack.com/p/residuality-theory)

## 3. Adjacent grounding material

### 3a. Statistical-significance experiments

The book page and external summaries repeat the claim that residuality experiments have shown a “statistically significant effect,” but I did not find a standalone published experimental paper in the sources searched. The closest adjacent research article is O’Reilly’s 2022 Procedia paper, “Residuality Theory, random simulation, and attractor networks,” which frames the theory in complexity-science terms and cites his earlier 2021 Philosophies paper. However, the search results did not expose a separate methods/results paper that clearly contains the underlying experimental design or significance test. So, as of the searched material, the empirical claim appears referenced but not transparently unpacked in an openly accessible source. [leanpub](https://leanpub.com/residuality)

### 3b. Longer-version book

I found no separate “longer version” of *Residues* in the searched results. The public Leanpub page for the book exists, and a 2025 Leanpub podcast listing also points back to Barry O’Reilly and residuality, but neither result identifies a subsequent expanded edition or a traditional publisher release. The available public material still points to the 2024 Leanpub book as the main book-length artifact. No alternate ISBN, revised edition, or new publisher imprint surfaced in the searches performed. [leanpub](https://leanpub.com/podcasts/leanpub?page=2)

### 3c. Cutter pieces (paywalled — metadata only)

I found metadata, not full text, for the Cutter Consortium series. The 2021 Cutter PDF collection page, *The Path to Residuality Theory*, identifies Barry M. O’Reilly as founder of Black Tulip Technology and creator of Antifragile System Design. Search snippets also confirm the existence of the Cutter pieces “No More Snake Oil” (2018), “There Is No Spoon” (2020), and “Hyperliminal Coupling” (2021), but the search results did not expose full text. Because those are paywalled, I did not attempt to retrieve the full articles. [github](https://github.com/oskardudycz/ArchitectureWeekly/blob/main/Summary.md)

### 3d. Talks and podcasts

I found several public talks and transcript-like sources. A 2022 YouTube session titled “An Introduction To Residuality Theory by Barry O’Reilly” describes him as a researcher and software architect and contains the core claim that “a random simulation of stress” is better than requirements or code iteration for producing architecture. A 2023 transcript on *software-architektur.tv* states that residuality models software as being stressed by “anything that’s outside your current model” and that the approach aims to yield “strong, resilient architectures”. A 2023 Avanscoperta blog transcript of his workshop describes residuality as combining “philosophy, the complexity sciences, and software engineering”. I also found 2025/2026 community listings for workshops and talks, including VirtualDDD sessions titled “An Introduction to Residuality Theory” and “Practical Residuality,” which suggest continued public presentation of the material. [virtualddd](https://virtualddd.com/sessions/an-introduction-to-residuality-theory-by-barry-m-oreilly/)

### 3e. Workshops and training

Public workshop material exists, though mostly as session descriptions rather than syllabi. The Avanscoperta post says their “Advanced Software Architecture Workshop” with O’Reilly was transcribed and positions residuality as a practical tool for navigating uncertainty. The VirtualDDD “Practical Residuality” listing says the session involves “hands on work with matrices” to test and validate architectures. These descriptions support the idea that O’Reilly’s practitioner-facing format compresses the theory into an incidence-matrix workflow and stressor simulation exercise. [virtualddd](https://virtualddd.com/sessions/practical-residuality-by-barry-m-oreilly/)

### 3f. Other people writing about residuality

Eric Normand is the clearest independent writer I found engaging the theory directly. A 2026 Reddit thread in r/ExperiencedDevs also shows a practitioner discussing having read the book and framing it as “interesting,” though that post does not amount to a formal review. A 2026 German blog post on doubleSlash describes residuality as a “future-proof software architecture” approach and summarizes it in practitioner language, but it reads more like an explanatory article than a critical engagement. ## 4. Open questions and gaps

The biggest gap remains the thesis itself: no repository record, defence metadata, supervisor, or abstract appeared in the searched sources. The second gap is the empirical claim about statistically significant experiments; public references exist, but I did not find the underlying study or analysis. Reviews are sparse outside Goodreads and one independent newsletter, and I found no strong critical essay from a major venue such as InfoQ, IEEE Software, CACM, or a prominent architecture blog in the material retrieved here. Finally, I found no explicit public treatment of Stacey or *Mille Plateaux* concepts, so those remain open as negative findings rather than confirmed absences. [software-architektur](https://software-architektur.tv/transcriptions/279.html)

## 5. Source notes

Search strategies that worked best were short, title-and-author queries around residuality, the book title, and known paper titles such as *The Machine in the Ghost* and *Residuality Theory, random simulation, and attractor networks*. Goodreads was useful for finding actual reader reactions, while Substack, workshop transcripts, and conference/session pages were useful for identifying practitioner-facing summaries. Search strategies that did not pay off were broad repository-style thesis searches and generic academic queries without exact title fragments; those mostly returned the known papers rather than a thesis record. Reliability varies: Goodreads gives direct review text but not strong editorial verification, while transcript and workshop pages are useful for verbatim claims but should be treated as venue-generated promotional material rather than independent analysis.
