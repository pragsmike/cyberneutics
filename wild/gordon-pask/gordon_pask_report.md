# Gordon Pask: A Deep Research Report

---

## Biography

Andrew Gordon Speedie Pask was born 28 June 1928 in Derby, England, the youngest child of Mary and Percy Pask. Percy was a partner in Pask, Cornish and Smart, a wholesale fruit business in Covent Garden. Pask had two much older brothers — Alfred, a Methodist minister, and Edgar, who became Professor of Anaesthetics — separated from him by roughly twenty years. The family moved to the Isle of Man when Pask was an infant, and he spent his early years there. He was frequently ill as a child, confined to bed for long stretches, and given little chance of reaching adulthood.¹

He was educated at Rydal Penrhos, a private school in North Wales, and obtained diplomas in geology from Bangor Technical College (later part of Bangor University) and mining engineering from Liverpool Polytechnic. Around 1949 he attended Downing College, Cambridge, graduating in 1952 with an MA in natural sciences (having studied medicine).²

### Cambridge and the Theatre Period

At Cambridge, Pask was better known for theatre than science. He met Robin McKinnon-Wood, who was studying mathematics and physics, and together they formed an entertainment company called Sirelelle, producing musical comedies. This theatrical collaboration turned out to be foundational: McKinnon-Wood later described their early theatrical ventures as "wholly consistent with the development of self-adaptive systems, self-organizing systems, man-machine interactions, etc." Pask designed and painted stage sets, wrote lyrics, and produced shows. Ted Nelson later described him as "an eloquent English leprechaun who dresses the Edwardian dandy."³

It was during this period that Pask became interested in cybernetics and information theory, catalysed by a presentation Norbert Wiener gave at Cambridge.²

### System Research Ltd.: 1953–c.1982

In 1953, Pask, together with Elizabeth Poole (whom he married in 1956; they had two daughters, Amanda and Hermione) and McKinnon-Wood, formally founded **System Research Ltd.** in Richmond, Surrey. The company became the institutional vehicle for virtually all of Pask's major work over the next three decades. It was registered as a non-profit in 1961. Pask was its director of research for thirty years.¹ ²

Funding came from the US Air Force, the UK Ministry of Defence, the Department of Employment, and the Social Science Research Council. Research topics included skill acquisition, learning styles and strategies, learning in groups, knowledge and task analysis, design processes, decision-making, problem-solving, and "learning to learn."¹

The company's basement laboratory in Richmond-upon-Thames was, by Pangaro's account, a remarkable environment: old and cannibalized machines, no housekeeping, damp — but full of revelations. Pask's suitcases typically contained "tobacco tins, clothes, electrics" and papers.⁴

**Key projects of the System Research period include:**

- **MusiColour (early 1950s)**: A light show in which coloured lights responded to musical keyboard input but would reduce their responsiveness to repeated patterns, forcing the musician to vary their playing. Demonstrated to Billy Butlin's organisation; it "exploded in a cloud of white smoke" during the demonstration but was restarted, prompting Butlin to remark that any machine that could survive such an explosion must be reliable.²

- **SAKI — Self-Adaptive Keyboard Instructor (1956)**: An adaptive teaching machine for training teleprinter operators, adjusting difficulty based on the learner's performance. One of the earliest adaptive educational technologies.¹

- **Electrochemical devices (mid-1950s to late 1950s)**: See the section on electrochemical devices below.

- **Fun Palace (1960s)**: A collaboration with architect Cedric Price and theatre director Joan Littlewood. Pask served as "resident cybernetician," introducing the concept of underspecified goals to architectural systems.⁵

- **Colloquy of Mobiles (1968)**: Exhibited at Jasia Reichardt's *Cybernetic Serendipity* at the Institute of Contemporary Arts, London. Physically constructed embodiment of Conversation Theory: suspended mechanical artefacts — "females" directing light beams, "males" using servos and mirrors to reflect light back. Left alone, they engaged in elaborate choreography; visitors could intervene with torches, but the system responded on its own terms. Designed by Yolanda Sonnabend, built by Mark Dowson and Tony Watts. "Popular when it worked, but a mite unreliable" (Mallen).² ⁵

- **CASTE — Course Assembly System and Tutorial Environment (late 1960s–1970s)**: An educational technology system with three panels — a communication console for topic selection, a modelling facility for demonstrations and explanations, and BOSS (Belief and Opinion Sampling System) for recording uncertainty. Students could construct their own entailment meshes.⁶ ⁷

- **THOUGHTSTICKER**: The first software implementation of entailment meshes. See the dedicated section below.

- **Ecogame (1970)**: A system dynamics model of a hypothetical national economy designed by Mallen and others, influenced by Pask's pedagogical ideas, encouraging participants to reflect on their own behaviour.²

### Academic Appointments

Pask earned a **PhD in psychology** from the University of London in 1964. In 1968–69, he became one of the **founding Professors of the Cybernetics Department at Brunel University**, where he held his position (part-time) until his death. The department had originally been intended as a research institute spearheaded by media proprietor Cecil Harmsworth King (influenced by Stafford Beer's management consulting work), but King died before its opening, so Brunel became primarily a postgraduate teaching department. Pask could not reconcile his System Research work with full-time duties, so Frank George became the full-time department head while Pask remained part-time.²

He obtained a **DSc in cybernetics from the Open University** in 1974 for his contribution to educational technology, while serving as visiting professor of educational technology there (1974–1979).¹ ²

### Dissolution and Later Years: c.1978–1996

Around 1978, Pask became more involved in Ministry of Defence projects, but struggled to keep System Research viable. The company disbanded in the early 1980s. He then taught at **Concordia University** in Montreal, the **University of Amsterdam** (Centre for Innovation and Co-operative Technology), and the **Architectural Association** in London, where he supervised doctoral students including Ranulph Glanville and organised lecture series.² ⁸

It was during the Amsterdam period that Conversation Theory was further refined into **Interaction of Actors (IA) theory**, a generalisation accounting for spontaneous, unstructured conversations and collective action beyond the constrained classroom settings CT was originally designed for.⁶ ⁹

Pask semi-retired on his 65th birthday (28 June 1993) and founded **Pask Associates**. In 1995, he received his third doctorate — an **ScD from Downing College, Cambridge**, one of very few awarded by the college. He also won a prize from **Old Dominion University** for his Complementarity Principle. He died on 29 March 1996 at the London Clinic, after battling serious illness for several years.¹ ² ⁹

Heinz von Foerster called him "Mister Cybernetics, the Cybernetician's Cybernetician" — though Pask preferred the term "Cybernetist" to evoke the physical nature of his IA theory.⁹

### Character

Pangaro's obituary captures the man: "Over the course of his nearly-68 years, he stayed up for 36-hour days, published 6 books and 270 papers, soldered machines into behaving like learning organisms, and developed a comprehensive theory of human cognition." He was "a man of the theatre, a man of style, intellect, and difficulty." His commitment to completeness — in theory, in talks, and in sentence structure — was a source of frustration for his audiences, who typically understood perhaps 10% of what he said, the rest being "equally important." He "lived pharmaceutically," worked nights, and was not always good at conversation despite authoring Conversation Theory.³

## The MIT Connection

The Pask–Negroponte–MIT relationship is real and significant, but its precise nature requires careful characterisation.

### The Architecture Machine Group

Nicholas Negroponte founded the **Architecture Machine Group** at MIT in 1968. The group explored computer-aided design, human-machine dialogue, and responsive environments. In 1985, it became the **MIT Media Lab**.¹⁰

Negroponte's vision was deeply influenced by Pask's cybernetics. In *Soft Architecture Machines* (MIT Press, 1975), Negroponte described the design process as a "dialogue" between human and machine — "so intimate — even exclusive — that only mutual persuasion and compromise would bring about ideas, ideas unrealizable by either conversant alone." The language of conversation, personalization, and adaptive interaction was directly Paskian.¹⁰

### Pask's Role

Pask wrote the **introduction** to *Soft Architecture Machines* — a chapter titled **"Aspects of Machine Intelligence"** — providing the theoretical framework for the book's opening section.¹¹ ¹²

Usman Haque states that "In the 1970s, Pask's contribution to the philosophy of MIT's Architecture Machine Group was focused around the notion of architecture as an enabler of collaboration."⁵ The Emerald journal account confirms that "In the 1970s, these two men — Pask and Negroponte — were the major proponents of cybernetics or computation in architecture."¹³

Negroponte adopted Pask's notion of personalisation under his own phrase, **"idiosyncratic computers"** — a concept directly traceable to Pask's insistence that knowledge and learning are inherently individual, not standardised.⁴

The lab worked with Pask to construct a **research proposal submitted to the US National Science Foundation**, titled **"Graphical Conversation Theory"**, which proposed merging the group's computer graphics capabilities with Pask's conversational framework. It was graphically designed; reviewers were split between finding it brilliant and disorganised. The proposal was declined.⁴

### Pangaro's Account

Paul Pangaro, who became Pask's most devoted student and later collaborator, describes meeting Pask at MIT in 1976: "I had heard well ahead that Pask was coming to consult with the MIT lab that I had joined after completing an undergraduate degree." He walked into Negroponte's office to find Pask "standing at a desk, looking down at papers with his head tilted while he ignited and drew deeply from his peculiar metal pipe. Nicholas introduced me to Pask as an actor, and introduced Pask as a producer and writer for the stage."⁴

A YouTube description of a 2024 talk states: "Paul Pangaro ran into Cybernetics head-on when he met Gordon Pask at MIT in 1976."¹⁴

### Assessment

Pask's role at MIT was that of a **consultant and intellectual influence** on Negroponte's Architecture Machine Group, not a faculty member or lab founder. He did not "start a lab" there — the Architecture Machine Group was Negroponte's creation. But Pask's ideas were foundational to the group's philosophy of human-machine conversation, adaptive systems, and personalised interaction. Given that the Architecture Machine Group became the Media Lab, Pask's intellectual influence on what became one of the world's most famous research institutions is documentable and substantial — mediated through Negroponte's direct adoption of Paskian concepts and through the introduction he wrote for the group's key publication.

*(Note: The claim that Pask "started a lab at MIT that was the forerunner of the Media Lab" appears to be an overstatement of a genuine and important consulting/influence relationship. The documented record supports intellectual paternity of key concepts, not institutional co-founding.)*

## Entailment Meshes

This is the core representational structure of Conversation Theory — a dynamic, evolving, subjective model of knowledge organised as a network of mutually entailing topic relations.

### What Entailment Meshes Represent

An entailment mesh (EM) is a representation of the topics and their relationships that emerge from a learner's or participant's activity in a conversational domain. It is a subset of the **entailment structure (ES)**, which represents all the possible topics that could be explored (the "expert knowledge" of a domain).⁶ ⁷

Key formal properties:

- **Topics** are the basic units — named elements in the object language L. A topic might be "sky," "color," "biology," "the operon," etc.
- **Topic relations** (R_i) within a conversational domain R must **mutually entail each other through cyclic reciprocation**. That is, all topics within an area of a domain are linked by relations of coherence — understanding one requires and supports understanding the others.⁷ ²⁰
- Topics are grouped into **relations** of different kinds: **analogies** (structural similarities between topics across different domains or sub-domains) and **coherences** (links showing how topics within a domain support each other). These relations comprise **concepts**.⁴ ⁷
- A **concept** in CT is not a static label but a **non-localised process** — the production, reproduction, and maintenance of a given topic relation via other topic relations. Formally: a concept is an ongoing feedback process producing, maintaining, and modulating a set of topics. The formal notation: R_H = PROC(R_H), where the head topic relation is identical to its procedural working model, and CON ≜ PROC = ⟨PROG, INTER⟩ where INTER is an interpretation function and PROG is a calculus.²⁰
- Entailment meshes represent **snapshots of a constantly evolving state of knowing** — they are dynamic, not fixed. The "dual" of the entailment mesh is the **architecture of conversations**, which captures the structure of interactions across perspectives necessary for the creation of these states of knowing.⁴

### How Entailment Meshes Are Constructed

Entailment meshes are built through an **elicitation process** — a structured conversation between a participant and the system (or between participants mediated by the system). The process involves:⁴ ⁶ ⁷

1. **Identifying topics**: The participant names or selects a topic.
2. **Providing explanations**: The participant states what is significant about the topic.
3. **Making distinctions**: The participant identifies which other topics are relevant to the explanation, thereby creating links in the mesh.
4. **Instantiating new topics**: When a mentioned topic doesn't yet exist in the mesh, it is created.
5. **Forming concepts**: When a sufficient set of topic relations has been established, a concept is instantiated — the grouping of topics linked by a statement.
6. **Detecting contradictions**: When a new statement conflicts structurally with an existing one, the system identifies the potential contradiction.
7. **Resolving contradictions**: Through a dialogue of further distinctions — are the conflicting terms synonyms? Is the shared topic different in each context? Can additional topics disambiguate?

In **CT (the formal, constrained version)**, the EM arises in interaction with a prescribed ES — the expert knowledge structure defines what topics exist, and the learner abstracts their own EM from this ES. In **IA (the later, generalised version)**, EMs are built bottom-up iteratively as P-individuals interact, with no fixed ES.⁶

### The THOUGHTSTICKER Illustration

The best concrete illustration of how entailment meshes are constructed in practice comes from Pangaro's account of the **THOUGHTSTICKER** system, built c.1985–86 in LISP on Symbolics hardware.⁴

THOUGHTSTICKER was a software environment based on entailment meshes that functioned as an adaptive, personalised information browser and authoring tool — predating the World Wide Web by a decade.

Here is a condensed version of the elicitation dialogue Pangaro documents, showing how two users build conflicting structures:

**User 'GP' creates initial structure:**

| THOUGHTSTICKER | User 'GP' | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Describe a topic | sky | Instantiates 'sky' as topic |
| What is significant about 'sky'? | The color of the sky is blue. | Records statement |
| What topics do you wish to distinguish? | color | Instantiates 'color' |
| The topic 'color' is unknown; explain? | Yes → "Reflected light of a particular wavelength..." | Records explanation |
| Further topics to distinguish? | blue | Instantiates 'blue' |
| Further topics? | None | Forms concept: {color, sky, blue} |

**User 'RG' enters conflicting knowledge:**

| THOUGHTSTICKER | User 'RG' | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Describe a topic | sky | Finds existing 'sky' |
| What is significant about 'sky'? | The color of the sky is red. | Records statement |
| Further topics? | red | Instantiates 'red' |
| Further topics? | None | Detects potential contradiction with {color, sky, blue} |
| Your statement may conflict with GP's: "The color of the sky is blue." Keep? | Yes | |
| Are 'blue' and 'red' synonyms? | No | |
| Is 'color' different in each? | No | |
| Is 'sky' different in each? | No | |
| Any topics to add? | Yes → Mars | Instantiates 'Mars' |
| Modify your statement? | Yes → "The color of the sky is red on Mars." | Forms concept: {color, sky, red, Mars} |

The contradiction detection operated **not by parsing the semantics of natural language text** but by analysing the **structural relationships in the entailment mesh** — comparing the topology of concept groupings. THOUGHTSTICKER contained a large logic tree for handling the various resolution paths.⁴

### Features of THOUGHTSTICKER

Beyond basic elicitation, THOUGHTSTICKER provided:⁴

- **Personalised navigation**: Dynamic links that varied by user, based on navigation history, learning style, and uncertainty measures. Roughly 20 uncertainty dimensions were tracked (conceptual, perceptual, etc.) and regulated to maximise learning.
- **Authoring aids**: Active prompts for consistency and completeness — checking implications, detecting similar statements by other authors, flagging contradictions.
- **Multi-author support**: Individual author identities kept distinct. The system could say: "A similar statement was made by so-and-so author on such-and-such a date; would you like to see that statement?"
- **Pre-Web browser functionality**: THOUGHTSTICKER had the look-and-feel of modern web browsers a decade before the Web, with the added advantage of an **organising principle for hyperlinks** — something Pangaro argues the Web still lacks.

### How Pask Used Entailment Meshes

- **CASTE**: Students explored an entailment structure (ES) openly; the system prompted demonstrations until mastery (indicated by a green light). Students could construct their own entailment meshes, making the course assembly function specialised to each user.⁶ ⁷
- **THOUGHTSTICKER**: As described above — knowledge elicitation, authoring, personalised browsing, contradiction detection.⁴
- **The Spy Ring History test**: A test of learning styles in which participants learned the history of a fictitious espionage network — five spies, three countries, five years. The comprehension component tested learning of similarities and differences between networks (holist tendency); the operation component tested learning of roles and action sequences (serialist tendency). Administration took one to two hours. Results correlated significantly with learning strategies observed in CASTE and INTUITION. Tested on students at Henley Grammar School, the Architectural Association, and other institutions (over 155 students total). It was noted that the test may have been biased toward STEM students.¹⁹ ²⁰
- **Educational research on serialist/holist styles**: Entailment meshes provided the representational substrate for Pask's discovery that learners use fundamentally different strategies — **serialists** who progress sequentially through topic relations, and **holists** who seek higher-order relations and the big picture. **Versatile** learners can do both. Matched instruction (holist strategy for holist learners, serialist for serialist) strongly favoured learning; mismatched instruction "completely disrupted" it.¹⁹ ⁷

### The Dual: Architecture of Conversations

Entailment meshes represent states of knowing; their **dual** is the **architecture of conversations**, which captures the **structure of the interactions** across perspectives that are necessary and inherent in creating those states. "To evolve from one state to another ('to learn') involves the exchange of information across perspectives represented in the conversation. To model any learning, from rat-in-a-maze path minimisation to team problem solving, entailment meshes plus conversational interactions comprise a necessary and sufficient set of views of that learning."⁴

### Significance

Pangaro's core argument about the significance of entailment meshes:

> "There are two significant differences between today's Web browsers and THOUGHTSTICKER circa 1986. First, entailment meshes represent an organising principle for the structure of content in information design. Instead of the pure anarchy of arbitrary hyperlinks across Web pages, THOUGHTSTICKER provides a reason for composing specific content in a single page-view... Second, [the adaptive, personalised nature based on individual learning style]."⁴

Entailment meshes treat knowledge as **dynamic, subjective, produced and reproduced through interaction** — not as static retrieval from a database. They represent what Pangaro calls "the kinetic recomputation of individualised knowing," as opposed to the "artificial intelligence" model of retrieving pre-stored structure.⁴

## Conversation Theory: The Broader Formal Apparatus

### P-Individuals and M-Individuals

Pask's ontology distinguishes two fundamental kinds of individual:⁶

- **M-individuals (mechanical individuals)**: Any system with material presence — a body, a brain, a computer, hardware. They act as the substrate that embodies P-individuals.
- **P-individuals (psychological individuals)**: Conceptual operators — analogous to software — comprising concepts, topics, or rule systems (e.g., "the logic for redistributing goods," "the principles of Euclidean geometry"). Multiple M-individuals can engage in joint activity processing a P-individual, and one M-individual can host multiple P-individuals.

This distinction is critical: cognition is unbounded because P-individuals (conceptual structures) can be processed by various mechanical bodies. A "conversation" in CT is fundamentally an interaction between P-individuals, whether embodied in humans, machines, or both.⁶

### Language Levels

Conversation Theory operates across multiple language levels:²⁰

- **L⁰**: Concerned with "how to *do* a topic" — procedural knowledge (recognise it, construct it, maintain it).
- **L¹**: Concerned with "explaining or justifying what a topic *means* in terms of other topics" — declarative knowledge.
- **L = ⟨L⁰, L¹⟩**: The object language as an ordered pair of these levels.
- **Lp (Protologic)**: A metalanguage that accompanies L, used for grouping topics and strategies, and allowing users to edit interfaces and operate upon entailment structures.⁶

### Teachback

The critical method of learning in CT. One participant teaches back to another what they have learned — demonstrating their understanding in their own terms. This closes the loop: the "teacher" can verify whether the "learner" has grasped the topic relation, and the act of teaching back itself consolidates and reveals the learner's understanding. Pask's experiments showed that teachback was significantly more effective than standard testing: requiring understanding rather than merely correct responses produced deeper, more transferable learning.⁶ ¹⁹ ²⁰

### The Cognitive Reflector

A **virtual machine** for selecting and executing concepts or topics from an entailment mesh shared by at least a pair of participants. It features an external **modelling facility** on which agreement between (say) teacher and pupil can be demonstrated by reproducing public descriptions of behaviour — for example, essay writing, report writing, or science "practicals."²⁰

### Serialist and Holist Strategies

Observed in Pask's experiments using CASTE, INTUITION, and the Spy Ring History test:¹⁹

- **Serialists**: Progress through topic structures sequentially, focusing on one topic at a time before moving to the next. Rely less on analogy relations. Prone to clustering exploratory transactions at the start of learning.
- **Holists**: Seek higher-order relations, the big picture. Assimilate information from many topics simultaneously. Typically have several goals and an aim ahead of them.
- **Versatile learners**: Can employ both strategies as appropriate. This is the ideal.

Mismatched instruction completely disrupted learning. The comprehension/operation style distinction (a broader categorisation than the serialist/holist strategy distinction observed in strict conversations) was: **comprehension learners** readily pick up an overall picture and redundancies; **operation learners** assimilate procedures and build concepts for isolated topics.¹⁹

### Evolution into Interaction of Actors (IA) Theory

CT was developed from 1972 as a framework for **goal-oriented conversations in constrained settings** — classrooms with strict rules, learning contracts, prescribed entailment structures.⁶

In the late 1980s and through the Amsterdam period, Pask took **the dual of the static "stick and ball" Conversation Theory**, producing the kinetic IA theory. Bernard Scott describes the shift: CT accounts for structured, prescribed-ES conversations (classrooms, training systems). IA **extends and generalises** CT to account for:⁶ ⁹

- Spontaneous, unstructured conversations and collective action (online gaming, traffic jams, social media)
- No exemplary rules or prescribed ES — conceptual repertoires of agents produce new operators bottom-up
- Emphasis on interaction, self/other reference, "imaging selves" (societal, personal, organisational)
- Attractions and repulsions that organise the interaction of actors

Per a letter Pask wrote (published in De Zeeuw, 2001), IA theory prioritised the dynamics of interaction over the static structures of knowledge representation.⁶ ⁹

## Electrochemical Devices

Distinct from entailment meshes (which are formal knowledge representations), Pask's electrochemical devices were **physical assemblages** built in the 1950s that demonstrated self-organisation and emergent concept formation in material substrates.

### Construction

- **Medium**: Aqueous solutions of metallic salts, primarily **ferrous sulphate (FeSO₄)**, in an acidic milieu (sulphuric or nitric acid). Experiments also involved other metals (iron, tin, silver) and catalysts like vanadium.²¹
- **Electrodes**: An array of **platinum electrodes** immersed in a dish containing the solution.
- **Current**: Passed between selected electrode pairs (transient via capacitative discharge or slowly changing source), steering the growth of dendritic metallic structures.²¹

### How Metallic Threads Grew

Passing current between selected electrodes caused **dendritic metallic threads** to grow in the solution. The threads formed fibrils resembling branching structures with gaps. They exhibited nerve-like properties (from prior "iron-wire" models): propagated electrical disturbances, refractoriness, thresholds, excitation/inhibition, oscillatory behaviour, saltatory conduction.²¹

### The Reward/Selection Mechanism

- **Reward form**: Electrical current and iron material allocated to structures that demonstrated useful conductance changes in response to environmental disturbances.
- **Dynamics**:
  1. Construction, reconstruction, and repair of parts (structural closure)
  2. Proliferation via branching/dendritic forms (increasing variety)
  3. Economic allocation: Finite resources created zero-sum competition — useful structures grew at the expense of non-useful ones
  4. Performance-contingent survival: Dynamic stabilisation of useful structures, de-stabilisation of non-useful ones
  5. Informationally open: Susceptible to perturbations from the environment²¹

### What They Demonstrated

In roughly **half a day**, the assemblages could evolve *de novo* sensitivity to either **sound** (discriminating ~50 Hz from ~100 Hz frequencies) or **magnetic fields**, depending on which environmental disturbance was rewarded. Fibrils physically resonated at the excitation frequency. The devices **adaptively constructed their own sensors**, choosing "relevance criteria" (observables) independent of the designer.²¹

Oliver Selfridge noted it was the second mechanism where "a machine build a machine electronically without any physical motion." Warren McCulloch wrote: "[Pask's] gadget does work; it does 'take habits' by a mechanism that Charles Peirce proposed."²

### Significance

Peter Cariani's analysis identifies the key distinction: contemporary neural networks and AI systems adapt mappings within **pre-specified** percept/action categories. Pask's devices **evolved the categories themselves** — automating sensor/effector design, achieving epistemic autonomy by constructing their own reference frames. This is a fundamentally different kind of adaptation, with implications for evolutionary robotics, machine creativity, and the limits of digital simulation (which lacks structural autonomy).²¹

Pask premiered the work at the **"Mechanisation of Thought Processes" conference** at the National Physical Laboratory, Teddington (1959), in a paper titled **"Physical Analogues to the Growth of a Concept."** Bernard Scott identifies this conference as a critical turning point in Pask's thinking — the physical metaphor of crystal growth as concept formation connected his engineering instincts to psychological theory.² ⁶

### Relationship to Entailment Meshes

The electrochemical devices and entailment meshes are distinct but share a deep conceptual kinship: both model **concept formation as an emergent, self-organising process** in which structures grow through interaction with an environment. The electrochemical threads are a physical analogue; entailment meshes are the formal, linguistic analogue. The metaphor of "growth" is deliberate in both — Pask's 1959 paper title was precisely "Physical Analogues to the Growth of a Concept."

## Contributions to Cybernetics

### What Is Distinctly Pask's

Pask took a **broad understanding** of cybernetics. Unlike physics, cybernetics had in his view no necessary commitment to a particular image of the environment. The focus was on observations made via observation — an inherently second-order stance. He defined cybernetics as **"the art of creating defensible metaphors"** — stressing its cross-disciplinary, analogical character.²

His distinctive contributions:

1. **Conversation as the fundamental unit of interaction and learning**. Where other cyberneticians modelled feedback loops in mechanical or biological systems, Pask made *conversation* — linguistic, reciprocal, multi-level exchange — the primitive. This was original: neither Ashby's homeostats, nor Beer's viable systems, nor even von Foerster's eigenforms placed conversation at the centre.

2. **The insistence that what is conserved in conversation is consciousness**. This is a strong ontological claim: consciousness is not a property of an isolated system but an emergent of conversational interaction.³

3. **Learning as the production and reproduction of concepts** — not the accumulation of information but the dynamic, feedback-governed process of building, testing, and rebuilding topic relations through teachback.

4. **The formal apparatus of CT**: P-individuals, M-individuals, entailment meshes, the L-levels, the protologic Lp — a complete formal framework for describing interaction, knowledge, and learning in a common notation applicable to human-human, human-machine, and machine-machine configurations.

5. **Physical computation**: The electrochemical devices as existence proofs that self-organising systems can evolve their own categories of perception — an idea distinct from and more radical than neural network weight-adaptation.

### Pask vs. Von Foerster

Von Foerster and Pask are both second-order cyberneticians — both insist the observer is part of the system. But von Foerster's framework is more **epistemological** (concerned with what we can know, eigenforms, the observer's construction of reality), while Pask's is more **interactional** (concerned with what happens *between* participants in a conversation, how shared understanding is achieved and tested). Von Foerster gives you the observer; Pask gives you the conversation between observers. They met at Namur in 1958 and remained allies, but their emphases are complementary, not identical.² ⁶

### Pask vs. Ashby

Ashby's cybernetics is about **variety** and **requisite variety** — the capacity of a controller to match the variety of disturbances it faces. Pask acknowledged Ashby's principles (his electrochemical devices implemented them), but Pask added **language, meaning, and subjectivity** to the picture. Ashby's systems are pre-linguistic; Pask's are fundamentally linguistic and conversational. Pask's systems don't just regulate variety — they negotiate meaning.²¹

### Pask vs. Beer

Beer's **Viable System Model** provides a structural account of organisational viability — recursion, autonomy, and meta-systemic control. Pask's CT provides a *process* account of how the entities within such structures learn, communicate, and reach shared understanding. They are complementary rather than competing: Beer gives you the architecture of a viable organisation, Pask gives you the conversational dynamics that animate it.

### Pask vs. Bateson

Both Bateson and Pask build **multi-level models of learning**. Bateson's levels (zero-learning, Learning I, Learning II/deutero-learning, Learning III) are anchored in logical types and metacommunication in natural interaction systems — often involving unconscious shifts in context. Pask's L-levels and serialist/holist strategies are more **explicitly epistemic and engineered** — about structuring educational conversations and conceptual networks in ways that can be implemented or simulated. Bateson gives you a phenomenology of "learning to learn" in living systems; Pask gives you a design for conversations and technologies that induce and manage those higher-order shifts.

### Influence on Architecture, Art, and Educational Technology

- **Architecture**: The Fun Palace (with Price and Littlewood); "The Architectural Relevance of Cybernetics" (1969); the introduction to Negroponte's *Soft Architecture Machines*; influence on John Frazer's work at the AA on evolutionary architecture; ongoing influence on responsive and adaptive architectural design.⁵ ⁸
- **Art**: Colloquy of Mobiles at Cybernetic Serendipity (1968); MusiColour; influence on Usman Haque's work (Moody Mushroom Floor, Open Burble, Paskian Environments).⁵
- **Educational technology**: SAKI, CASTE, INTUITION, THOUGHTSTICKER — adaptive systems that matched teaching strategy to individual learning style decades before "personalised learning" became a buzzword. The Encyclopædia Britannica entry on Cybernetics (1972).¹ ⁴ ⁹
