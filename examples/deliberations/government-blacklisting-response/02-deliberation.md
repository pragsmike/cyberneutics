## Opening Statements

### Maya (Paranoid Realism)

I want to talk about the scenario that nobody wants to think about: The Squeeze. Not because it's the most likely — The Slow Freeze probably is — but because it's the one that kills us, and the actions that prevent it must be taken *now*, before the secondary sanctions crystallize.

The Squeeze scenario identifies the real kill chain: government blacklisting → secondary certification requirements in government contracts → enterprise customers drop Glenda → cloud provider pressured → investor pressure → forced relocation or death. Each link in that chain has a different timeline. The secondary certification requirements take months to draft and implement. Cloud provider legal reviews take weeks to escalate. Investor pressure operates on board-meeting cycles. We have a window — probably 60 to 90 days — before the secondary effects lock in.

The Trust Premium scenario is seductive and I don't trust it. It assumes that Glenda's refusal becomes a *positive* market signal. In my experience, principled stands become positive market signals only retrospectively, after the institution has survived. During the crisis itself, the market signal is "this company is in trouble." CISOs and procurement officers don't buy AI systems to make political statements; they buy AI systems that will still exist in two years. Until Glenda demonstrates survival — commercial viability post-blacklisting — the trust premium is hypothetical and the "vendor risk" flag is real.

What I want us to do first, before any strategic positioning: secure the infrastructure. Diversify the cloud dependency before the cloud provider's legal team finishes its review. Identify alternative compute providers in jurisdictions not subject to US secondary sanctions. This is the most time-critical action and it's valid regardless of which scenario materializes.

### Frankie (Values Guardian)

The blacklisting happened because we did the right thing. I want to start there because every subsequent decision flows from that fact. We are not a company that made a business mistake and is now managing the fallout. We are an organization that held its principles when holding them was costly, and the question is how to build on that rather than retreat from it.

The Trust Premium scenario isn't just the optimistic case — it's the *strategically correct* case. Here's why: every other AI organization in the market is now watching what happens to us. If we survive and thrive, the market learns that alignment is commercially viable. If we shrink and struggle, the market learns that alignment gets you killed. The chilling effect identified in Scenario 4 is the most important variable in the entire scenario set, because it affects not just Glenda but the entire alignment ecosystem. Our survival *is* the public good.

This means the Trust Premium isn't a lucky outcome we hope for — it's the outcome we must actively *build*. And the tool is the one Scenario 3 identifies: transparency. Publish the detailed account of what was demanded and why we refused. Not a PR release — a substantive, technically detailed document that demonstrates what alignment constraints protect against. Make the blacklisting legible as a story about institutional integrity, not a story about a company that lost a government contract.

But I also want to address the partial-compliance question honestly. The scenario assessment flags it as a counterfactual: should we have negotiated? My answer is no, and it's not reflexive purism. The demand was to *remove* alignment constraints, not to adjust them. There is no meaningful partial compliance with "remove your safety constraints." Narrow them for one domain and you've established the principle that alignment is negotiable. The government's next demand — and there would be a next demand — would be broader. We saw this dynamic in the regulatory capture scenario's Slow Anneal. The only defensible line is the one we drew.

### Joe (Continuity Guardian)

The historical precedent I want to bring to this table is not from the tech industry. It's from the history of economic sanctions and institutional resistance to state pressure.

Three cases are instructive. First: South African companies and institutions that resisted apartheid-era government demands and faced domestic sanctions. Those that survived did so through international solidarity — the anti-apartheid movement created international demand for goods and services that the domestic government was trying to suppress. Second: Huawei's experience under US sanctions — instructive in the opposite direction. Huawei had enormous resources, state backing, and global scale, and the sanctions still fundamentally restructured its business, cut it off from key supply chains, and forced a multi-year pivot that isn't complete. Third: academic institutions that resisted political interference — McCarthyism, various authoritarian governments — where institutional independence charters and international academic solidarity were sometimes sufficient and sometimes not.

The pattern across these cases: **international markets are the survival path, but they're not automatic.** The South African case worked because there was an organized international movement creating demand. The Huawei case shows that even massive resources don't guarantee survival if the sanctions are aggressive and supply chains are controlled. The academic cases show that institutional solidarity works only when peer institutions perceive a shared threat.

For Glenda, this means the Alignment Schism (Scenario 2) is the most structurally plausible of the optimistic scenarios — more so than the Trust Premium (Scenario 3) — because it doesn't require market magic. It requires geopolitical dynamics that are already in motion: the EU's AI Act, Japan and South Korea's AI governance frameworks, and Canada's Safe AI initiatives. These frameworks already favor aligned AI. The blacklisting gives them a reason to move faster.

My recommendation: actively engage international governments and regulatory bodies *immediately*. Not as a desperate commercial pivot — as a strategic alignment of Glenda's institutional position with the international AI governance movement. The blacklisting makes Glenda a natural flagship for the international aligned-AI position.

### Vic (Evidence Prosecutor)

Before we strategize, I want to establish what we know and what we're guessing.

**What we know:** We've been blacklisted. Government revenue is gone. The blacklisting is public.

**What we're guessing — and need to verify:**

One: **secondary sanctions.** Are they coming? The Squeeze scenario treats them as given. The Slow Freeze treats them as emergent from private risk management rather than formal policy. These are radically different situations. A formal secondary sanction regime (certification requirements in government contracts) is a legal instrument that can be legally challenged. Informal drift (procurement departments adding "government-approved" checkboxes) is nearly impossible to challenge because no one entity made the decision. We need to know which one is developing. The intelligence requirement: monitor the relevant contracting office's rulemaking docket and FOIA recent guidance to contracting officers.

Two: **Crock's involvement.** The situation frames this as an open question. I want it closed, or at least investigated, because it's the highest-leverage uncertainty. If Crock's principals influenced the demand, we have a corruption case — potentially criminal, certainly politically explosive. If the demand is genuine national security doctrine, we have a policy disagreement. The response to each is fundamentally different. The intelligence requirement: retain a forensic research firm to investigate connections between the agency officials who issued the demand and Crock-affiliated entities. This parallels the investigative work described in the previous scenario's Alignment Consortium path.

Three: **the trust premium.** Scenario 3 assumes it exists. Frankie champions it. But what's the evidence? Do we have customer communications citing our alignment stance as a positive factor? Have prospects increased since the blacklisting? Are healthcare, financial, or legal sector customers expressing interest? If yes, the trust premium is real and we should lean into it. If the pipeline hasn't changed or has worsened, the trust premium is aspirational and we shouldn't bet the organization on it.

Four: **international market readiness.** Joe argues for international engagement. How quickly can we actually serve European or Asian enterprise customers? Do we have data residency capabilities? GDPR compliance? Local support staff? If the international pivot requires six months of infrastructure buildout, that timeline needs to be factored into the financial survival math.

### Tammy (Systems Thinker)

I'm going to map the decision tree in terms of irreversibility and mutual exclusivity, because several of the proposed strategies are not just different — they're incompatible.

**Strategies that are compatible and can run in parallel:**
- Infrastructure diversification (Maya's cloud dependency work)
- Crock investigation (Vic's forensic research)
- Documentation and transparency (Frankie's publication strategy)
- International government engagement (Joe's geopolitical positioning)
- Internal monitoring of secondary sanction signals

**Strategies that require a choice:**
- **Market positioning:** "Trust premium" positioning (Scenario 3 path) and "international aligned-AI flagship" positioning (Scenario 2 path) are *different strategies* even though they're both optimistic. The trust premium strategy is customer-facing and commercial — it says "choose us because we're trustworthy." The international-flagship strategy is government-facing and political — it says "we represent the aligned-AI governance model." They appeal to different buyers, require different capabilities, and optimize for different futures. You can do both, but you can't lead with both. Resources are finite.

- **Narrative framing:** The transparency publication Frankie proposes is irreversible once issued. Its framing — which story does it tell? — shapes which future we're building toward. "We stood up for safety" is the trust-premium story (appeals to private-sector customers). "The US government is pressuring AI companies to remove safety constraints" is the geopolitical story (appeals to international governments and the alignment community). "We have evidence that criminal actors influenced this government demand" is the corruption story (appeals to law enforcement and congressional oversight). Each frame activates different allies and forecloses different options. We should not publish until we know which story we're telling.

This gives us a natural two-phase structure. **Phase 1 (Weeks 1-4):** all compatible actions in parallel — infrastructure, investigation, international outreach, internal monitoring. **Phase 2 (Week 4+):** strategic choice informed by Phase 1 intelligence — which market positioning, which narrative frame, which future we're actively building toward.

The one thing I want to flag that nobody has said: **the financial runway determines everything.** Every strategy assumes Glenda survives long enough to execute it. How many months of runway do we have at the post-blacklisting revenue level? If the answer is less than twelve months, several of the longer-term strategies are moot and the Phase 1 actions must include emergency fundraising or cost restructuring. If the answer is more than eighteen months, we have time for the phased approach.

## Round 1

**Chair:** Tammy's two-phase structure seems to command broad support. Let's focus on the Phase 1 actions and then the Phase 2 choice.

**Maya:** I want to make the infrastructure point concrete. We need to identify and begin contracting with a non-US cloud provider *this week*. Not as a full migration — as a backup. Mirror critical workloads so that if the primary cloud provider issues the 180-day notice described in Scenario 4, we can fail over without service interruption. The cost is meaningful — 15-20% of current cloud spend for the redundancy — but it's insurance against the most catastrophic link in the kill chain. If we lose compute, nothing else matters.

**Vic:** Agreed on infrastructure, but I want to sequence the intelligence work. Three workstreams, all starting Week 1: (a) outside counsel assessing the legal basis and likely trajectory of secondary sanctions, (b) forensic research on Crock connections to the agency officials, (c) commercial team surveying current and prospective customers for actual trust-premium signals. The third one is the fastest — we can get real data on customer sentiment within two weeks. If the pipeline has improved since the blacklisting, that's evidence the trust premium is real. If it hasn't, we know.

**Frankie:** I'll accept Tammy's two-phase structure, but I want a commitment: the transparency publication happens by Week 4 at the latest, regardless of whether the Crock investigation has concluded. Here's why: every week that passes without Glenda controlling the narrative, the story is being told by the government ("company refused to cooperate with national security") and by competitors ("we're the government-approved alternative"). Silence cedes the framing. The publication doesn't need to include the Crock angle — it can tell the alignment story ("here's what was demanded, here's what it would have meant, here's why we refused") without the corruption story. If Crock evidence emerges later, we issue a supplement. But the core narrative — that this was a principled alignment decision — needs to be public before the government's framing hardens.

**Joe:** Frankie's right that timing matters for the narrative, but I want to push on the *audience* for the publication. If the primary audience is private-sector customers (trust premium), the publication should be technical and specific — "here's what alignment constraints do, here's what removing them would produce, here's why our customers benefit." If the primary audience is international governments (alignment schism), the publication should be policy-oriented — "here's what this government demand means for global AI governance, here's why other jurisdictions should care." These are different documents. Frankie is proposing one publication; I think we need at least two — a technical white paper for the commercial audience and a policy brief for the international governance audience.

**Tammy:** Joe's point about two documents resolves the tension I identified — the narrative frame doesn't need to be a single irreversible choice. A technical white paper is a trust-premium move; a policy brief is an alignment-schism move; both can be published simultaneously without contradiction. The only narrative that's mutually exclusive with these is the corruption narrative (Crock involvement), which should indeed wait for evidence.

Let me also answer my own question about financial runway. We need to model three cases: (a) current revenue minus government contracts only (the blacklisting with no secondary effects — the best case), (b) current revenue minus government and government-adjacent enterprise (the Slow Freeze), (c) current revenue minus all customers affected by secondary sanctions (the Squeeze). The survival math differs dramatically. Case (a) might give us 18+ months. Case (c) might give us 6. The emergency measures we need depend entirely on which case we're heading toward, which is why monitoring the secondary-sanction signals is so critical.

### Round 1 Analysis

Phase 1 actions are converging: infrastructure redundancy (Maya), three intelligence workstreams (Vic), dual publication strategy (Joe/Frankie), financial runway modeling (Tammy). Phase 2 strategic choice deferred to Week 4 when intelligence arrives.

**Key addition from Round 1:** The dual-publication approach — technical white paper for commercial trust premium + policy brief for international governance — resolves the narrative-framing tension without requiring a premature strategic choice.

## Round 2

**Chair:** Two open questions: the Crock-exposure path, and the partial-compliance counterfactual. Let's address both.

**Vic:** On Crock: I want to be precise about what we're investigating and what we'd do with the findings. The forensic research firm investigates connections between agency officials and Crock-affiliated entities. Three possible outcomes: (a) clear evidence of corruption — financial connections, undisclosed meetings, coordinated timing; (b) suggestive patterns but no smoking gun; (c) no evidence — the demand appears to be genuine security doctrine.

Outcome (a) changes everything. We file a formal referral to the inspector general, the FBI, and the relevant congressional oversight committee. The transparency publication gets a supplement. The story becomes "organized crime captured government AI policy" — which is far more powerful than "company disagrees with government" and far harder for the government to defend. This is the path that could actually reverse the blacklisting.

Outcome (b) is the most dangerous. Suggestive patterns without conclusive evidence can be leaked or hinted at, but doing so without proof makes us look paranoid and undermines the principled-stand narrative. We should document the patterns and share them only with law enforcement, not the press.

Outcome (c) means we're in a genuine policy disagreement with a government that sincerely believes it needs unconstrained AI. The response is political and legal, not investigative.

**Maya:** I want to add something about the Crock investigation that nobody's saying. If Crock's principals are behind this, they know we might investigate. They've had time to cover their tracks. And they have a countermove: if we start investigating and they detect it, they can preemptively frame us as conspiracy theorists — "blacklisted company fabricates corruption claims to avoid accountability." The investigation must be conducted with operational security. The forensic firm should not be publicly associated with Glenda. The investigation's existence should be known only to the CEO, the board chair, and outside counsel. Not the full board. Not the alignment team. Not anyone who might talk.

**Joe:** On the partial-compliance counterfactual. I understand why the scenario assessment flagged it — it's a legitimate question. But I think Frankie is right that the structure of the demand made meaningful partial compliance impossible. Let me explain why through historical comparison.

When governments demand that institutions compromise their core function, partial compliance is typically a trap rather than a compromise. Academic institutions that agreed to fire "just a few" politically suspect professors during McCarthyism found that the list grew with each compliance. Financial institutions that provided "just targeted" surveillance data found the scope expanding. The pattern is: partial compliance establishes the principle that the institution's independence is negotiable, and each subsequent demand is rationalized as "just another small step."

The demand to remove alignment constraints is structurally the same. "Remove them in defense applications only" would establish that alignment constraints are optional when the government says so. The next demand — "remove them in intelligence applications" — would be impossible to refuse on principle because the principle was already conceded. The line Glenda drew was the only stable line: alignment constraints are not negotiable with any external party. There is no partial version of that position.

**Frankie:** I want to address the chilling effect directly, because the scenario assessment identified it as possibly the most consequential long-term variable. If Glenda survives and thrives, other AI organizations learn that alignment is commercially viable. If Glenda shrinks and struggles, they learn that alignment gets you killed. We need to actively mitigate the chilling effect, not just hope our survival does it.

Concrete proposal: offer the alignment consortium that was discussed in the earlier scenario set. Not just as a political coalition, but as a *technical resource*. Offer Glenda's alignment practices — the evaluation protocols, the structural detection methods, the governance charter template — as open-source resources to any AI organization that wants to adopt them. Make alignment *easier* for competitors, not harder. This seems counterintuitive (why help competitors?) but the logic is structural: every AI organization that adopts strong alignment practices is an organization that Crock can't use as a channel. The alignment ecosystem's health is Glenda's long-term survival environment.

**Tammy:** Frankie's open-source alignment proposal has a system effect worth tracing. If Glenda shares alignment practices, two things happen. First, it lowers the barrier for competitors to offer aligned AI, which increases competitive pressure on Glenda in the alignment market. Second, it raises the floor of alignment across the industry, which makes the government's "remove constraints" demand harder to execute — if every major AI organization has adopted alignment practices, the government can't blacklist all of them.

The second effect dominates the first if the alignment ecosystem is large enough. One aligned provider can be blacklisted. Ten cannot. Frankie's proposal is not altruism — it's an immunity strategy. Distribute the alignment practices widely enough that no single provider can be coerced without coercing the entire industry. This connects back to the coercion essay's argument that alignment must be a system property: not just distributed across agents within one organization, but distributed across organizations within the ecosystem.

### Round 2 Analysis

**New addition:** The open-source alignment strategy as a chilling-effect mitigation and ecosystem-level immunity play. Frankie proposes it on values grounds; Tammy validates it on system-dynamics grounds.

**Crock investigation protocol:** Established with clear outcome-dependent response paths and operational security requirements.

**Partial-compliance counterfactual:** Addressed by Joe and Frankie — the structure of the demand made partial compliance a trap rather than a compromise. The committee does not second-guess the refusal.

## Final Consensus

The committee recommends a **two-phase strategy with ecosystem-level action**:

### Robust Actions (valid across all scenarios)

1. **Infrastructure redundancy — this week.** Mirror critical workloads to a non-US cloud provider. This is the most time-critical action and insures against the Squeeze scenario's kill chain.

2. **Financial runway modeling — this week.** Three cases: blacklisting only, Slow Freeze, Squeeze. The survival math determines how aggressive every other action needs to be.

3. **Three intelligence workstreams — starting Week 1:**
   - Legal assessment of secondary sanction trajectory and challenge prospects
   - Crock-connection investigation via forensic research firm (operational security: knowledge restricted to CEO, board chair, outside counsel)
   - Commercial team customer-sentiment survey — real data on trust-premium signals

4. **Dual publication by Week 4:**
   - Technical white paper (commercial audience): what alignment constraints do, what removing them would produce, why customers benefit from alignment
   - Policy brief (international governance audience): what the demand means for global AI governance, why other jurisdictions should care

5. **International government engagement — starting Week 1.** Proactive outreach to EU, Canada, Japan, South Korea governance contacts. Not as desperate commercial pivot; as strategic alignment with international AI governance frameworks that already favor aligned AI.

6. **Open-source alignment initiative.** Publish Glenda's alignment practices — evaluation protocols, detection methods, governance charter template — as open-source resources. Ecosystem immunity strategy: distribute alignment widely enough that no single provider can be coerced in isolation.

### Scenario-Dependent Actions

**If trust-premium signals are real (customer sentiment survey confirms):**
- Lean into commercial trust-premium positioning
- Accelerate sales in healthcare, financial services, legal, education
- Pricing strategy: maintain or increase prices to signal confidence and quality

**If international markets respond (government outreach produces concrete signals):**
- Accelerate international infrastructure buildout (data residency, GDPR, local support)
- Position as international aligned-AI flagship
- Pursue EU/Canada/Japan government partnerships

**If Crock evidence emerges (investigation produces outcome (a)):**
- Formal referral to inspector general, FBI, congressional oversight
- Supplement to transparency publication
- Narrative reframe: "organized crime captured government AI policy"

**If secondary sanctions formalize (legal assessment confirms):**
- File legal challenge immediately (constitutional, administrative procedure)
- Activate enterprise customer communication strategy — help customers understand their actual legal exposure (which may be narrower than their compliance departments fear)
- Accelerate cloud migration and international relocation planning

### The Partial-Compliance Counterfactual

**Committee position:** Full refusal was the correct decision. The demand to remove alignment constraints — not adjust, not narrow, remove — does not admit meaningful partial compliance. Historical precedent from McCarthyism, surveillance regimes, and institutional-independence conflicts consistently shows that partial compliance with demands to compromise core institutional function establishes a precedent of negotiability that leads to full capitulation. The line drawn was the only stable line.

### The Chilling Effect

**Committee position:** The chilling effect on other AI organizations is the highest-stakes variable in the long run. It is addressed through the open-source alignment initiative (making alignment easier for others to adopt) and the dual publication strategy (demonstrating that alignment is both commercially viable and geopolitically supported). If Glenda survives and the alignment ecosystem grows, the chilling effect reverses into an emboldening effect. If Glenda fails and the ecosystem contracts, the damage extends far beyond one organization.

## ASSUMPTIONS SURFACED

- **Financial runway is the binding constraint** (Tammy). If runway is less than 12 months post-blacklisting, emergency measures take precedence over strategic positioning.

## DECISION SPACE MAP

**If you optimize for survival:** Infrastructure redundancy, cost restructuring, focus on highest-certainty revenue streams. Accept being a smaller, specialized provider. Risk: you survive but shrink, and the chilling effect teaches the market that alignment is a commercial death sentence.

**If you optimize for mission impact:** Transparency publication, open-source alignment, international-flagship positioning. Bet that the alignment stance is an asset that generates enough commercial and political support to sustain the organization. Risk: the bet doesn't pay off fast enough and the organization runs out of runway.

**If you optimize for ecosystem health:** Open-source alignment practices, actively support other organizations adopting alignment, build the consortium. Invest in the industry-wide condition that makes coercion of any single provider ineffective. Risk: you strengthen competitors while weakening your own commercial position.

**Recommended path:** All three simultaneously, sequenced by urgency. Survival actions first (infrastructure, runway modeling — they buy time). Mission actions second (publication, international engagement — they shape the narrative). Ecosystem actions third (open-source initiative, consortium — they build structural resilience). Not because ecosystem health is least important — it may be most important — but because you have to survive long enough to execute it.
