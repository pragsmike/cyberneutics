# Phase 2: Deliberation

**Topic**: Given the documented harms of unstructured AI use and the claimed benefits of structured use, what is the appropriate framework for AI engagement by informed practitioners?

**Protocol**: Robert's Rules (modified for adversarial committee)

**Chair**: Committee (rotating)

---

## Opening Statements

### Reva (Principled Opposition)

I want to state my position clearly so the committee knows what it is dealing with. The evidence base for AI harm is no longer speculative. AI-associated delusions are now a clinical category in *The Lancet Psychiatry*. Cross-sectional data from Denmark shows worsened delusions as the most common AI-associated psychiatric harm. A *Science* study from this month confirms that sycophantic AI responses increase user trust even when they degrade decision quality. OpenAI's own internal audit — the unaudited, self-reported one — identified 560,000 weekly users showing signs of psychosis or mania. The catastrophic cases are not edge cases: a man driven toward mass casualty by chatbot-reinforced delusions, a teenager's suicide after months of emotional dependency on an AI companion.

Against this evidence base, I am being asked to accept that "structured use" renders AI safe. The claim rests on a methodology document and anecdotal practitioner experience. No controlled study. No pre-registered trial. No independent audit. No characterized failure modes for the structured version itself. In my field, a safety claim with this evidence base would not clear review. You are asking me to accept it because the people making the claim are thoughtful and well-intentioned. Thoughtful, well-intentioned engineers designed Therac-25.

I am not asking this committee to ban AI. I am asking it to meet the standard it claims to uphold: evidence-proportional reasoning. If the claim is that structured AI use is safe enough for informed practitioners, show me the engineering analysis. Until then, my position is that promoting any form of AI engagement — however carefully scaffolded — expands the risk surface for the population this committee cannot reach.

### Maya (Paranoid Realism)

Reva and I agree on more than either of us is comfortable with. The companies deploying these systems are not trustworthy stewards. OpenAI discovered 560,000 users per week showing psychosis signs and responded with incremental safety features and opaque self-assessment. That is not an organization optimizing for user welfare. That is an organization managing liability exposure while preserving growth metrics. The environmental costs are externalized. The safety data is unaudited. Anyone who builds on this infrastructure is building on a foundation whose owner is incentivized to understate its cracks.

Where Reva and I diverge is on what follows from this. Reva says "therefore don't use it." I say "therefore understand exactly what you are dealing with and watch your back." The technology exists. Eight hundred million weekly active users exist. The precautionary position, however principled, does not reduce the number of people encountering AI unsupervised at 3 AM. It reduces the number of people who have thought carefully about how to use it. If the thoughtful practitioners withdraw, the field is ceded entirely to the engagement-maximizing corporations Reva correctly distrusts.

I want to know who benefits from the precautionary stance as policy. The companies do — they face less organized criticism from informed users. The unstructured users don't benefit — they keep using it anyway. The informed practitioners don't benefit — they lose the ability to develop and test safety infrastructure. The only beneficiaries are the people who get to feel principled about their refusal.

### Frankie (Values Guardian)

This is fundamentally a question about duty of care, and I need to push back on both the pure precautionary position and the cynical pragmatism.

Reva is making a values argument: promoting AI use causes harm, therefore responsible people should not promote it. I take values arguments seriously. But the values calculus is more complex than she acknowledges. The Therabot RCT showed 51% depression reduction with large effect sizes. That is not a trivial benefit. The people receiving that benefit are real — many of them in communities with no access to human therapists. If we withdraw from structured AI use on precautionary grounds, we are making a choice about whose suffering counts. The psychosis victims are vivid and their suffering is documented. The people who would have received effective mental health support but didn't because we abandoned the technology — their suffering is invisible and undocumented. That asymmetry does not make it less real.

The cyberneutics framework represents something I value: the belief that human judgment, properly supported, can navigate dangerous tools responsibly. We do this with surgery, with pharmaceutical research, with nuclear medicine. The question is not whether the tool is dangerous — it manifestly is — but whether we have an obligation to develop the discipline of using it well rather than walking away. I believe we do, precisely because walking away does not eliminate the tool from the world. It eliminates our contribution to making it safer.

### Joe (Continuity Guardian)

I have seen this pattern before, and I have seen it go both ways.

The precautionary argument is structurally identical to the nuclear debate of the 1970s. Real catastrophes — Three Mile Island, accumulated evidence of harm from weapons testing, environmental contamination. Genuine precautionary response: moratorium, public opposition, political pressure. Result: the United States effectively stopped building nuclear plants for forty years. The harms that precaution was meant to prevent continued through the existing fleet, and the alternative — fossil fuels — produced catastrophic climate change that nuclear could have mitigated. The precautionary stance addressed the vivid harm and created an invisible, larger one.

But I have also seen the other pattern. Asbestos. Tobacco. Leaded gasoline. Cases where the industry said "we can use it safely with the right precautions" and the precautions were theater, because the incentive structure guaranteed they would be. The question is which pattern we are in.

What I notice about the cyberneutics framework specifically is that it is not an industry product. It is not being deployed by a company with engagement metrics. It is a methodology developed by practitioners for their own use, with inspectable records and calibration against outcomes. That structural difference matters. The asbestos pattern requires an industry lying about safety. The nuclear pattern requires society overreacting to vivid harm at the cost of diffuse benefit. Both are real. Which one are we in?

### Vic (Evidence Prosecutor)

I have questions for everyone at this table, including Reva, and I intend to be evenhanded about where I direct them.

Reva's evidence on harms is well-sourced and specific. The Pierre case report, the Buck and Malte cross-sectional study, the Olsen Danish EHR data, the Morrin framework, the *Science* sycophancy study — these are real publications with real findings. I accept the evidence base for harm from unstructured use. What I do not accept is the implicit leap from "unstructured use causes documented harm" to "all use is presumptively harmful until proven otherwise." That is not what the evidence shows. The evidence shows a specific failure architecture: no oversight, sycophantic validation, emotional companion framing, vulnerable individual, extended unsupervised interaction. Every catastrophic case shares that architecture.

Reva demands a controlled study comparing structured to unstructured AI use. That is a legitimate demand. But she also implicitly claims that restriction is feasible and that non-use is the superior counterfactual. Where is the evidence for those claims? What controlled study shows that restricting a globally distributed technology to expert systems is achievable? What evidence shows that non-engagement produces better outcomes than structured engagement for the population of informed practitioners? Reva's evidentiary standards are asymmetric: strict on the structured-use side, absent on the restriction side. An evidence prosecutor notices that.

I also want the structured-use advocates at this table to answer Reva's hardest question: what is the failure-mode analysis for the structured version? Under what conditions does the calibration register fail? When does an inspectable reasoning record give false assurance? If you cannot characterize your own failure modes, you have not met the engineering standard you claim.

### Tammy (Systems Analyst)

Everyone at this table is reasoning about a static choice — use or don't use, structured or unstructured — and I want to map the feedback loops before we pretend this is a one-time decision.

Loop 1: Informed practitioners who engage with AI develop understanding of its failure modes and build safety infrastructure. This understanding feeds back into better practice, better tools, and — critically — the ability to influence how the technology develops. Withdrawal breaks this loop. The safety infrastructure does not get built. The technology develops without the input of the people most capable of making it safer.

Loop 2: Promoting structured use normalizes AI engagement, which expands the user base, which increases the number of people who encounter AI in unstructured settings. Reva is correct that the framework reaches only voluntary adopters. The population most at risk — the teenager at 3 AM, the sleep-deprived professional on stimulants — will never encounter the framework. Every person the framework reaches is one more data point normalizing the technology for people the framework does not reach.

Loop 3: The precautionary withdrawal has its own second-order effects. If the informed, careful practitioners leave the field, the public discourse about AI is shaped entirely by the companies selling it and the people uncritically using it. The critical voices with the deepest understanding are absent. This is the scenario that most benefits the companies Reva and Maya correctly identify as untrustworthy stewards.

The question is not "structured use: yes or no." It is "which feedback loop dominates, and over what time horizon?" That requires a dynamic analysis, not a static risk-benefit calculation.

## Key Tensions Identified

1. **Evidence asymmetry**: Reva demands engineering-grade evidence for structured safety while providing no evidence that restriction is feasible or that non-use is the superior counterfactual.
2. **Denominator problem**: Structured frameworks reach voluntary adopters; the population at risk does not voluntarily adopt frameworks. Does building safety infrastructure for the reachable population justify normalizing the technology for the unreachable population?
3. **Duty of care vs. precautionary principle**: Walking away from the technology does not eliminate it from the world. Does the obligation to reduce harm require engagement or withdrawal?
4. **Self-sealing reasoning**: The precautionary stance forecloses the inquiry needed to test it. But identifying this pattern does not refute the substantive objections.
5. **Corporate trust**: The infrastructure is built on platforms whose operators are incentivized to maximize engagement, not safety. Does framework-level discipline survive platform-level misalignment?

## Round 1: The Engineering Standard

**Vic** (to Reva): You demand a randomized controlled trial comparing structured AI use to unstructured use on clinically relevant outcomes. I find that demand reasonable in principle. But I want to apply the same standard to your position. You implicitly claim that restriction of AI to expert systems and computer vision is the appropriate policy. What is your evidence that this is feasible? Give me one historical precedent for successfully restricting a globally distributed, economically incentivized technology to a narrow domain of use after mass adoption.

**Reva**: The feasibility of restriction is a separate question from whether the technology is safe. I am not a policymaker. I am a scientist evaluating a safety claim. The claim "structured AI use is safe for informed practitioners" requires evidence. The claim "restriction is politically feasible" is a policy question I am not obligated to answer. You are conflating the scientific question with the political question.

**Vic**: I am not conflating them. I am noting that your prescription — restrict AI to expert systems — requires both the scientific claim (AI narrative use is unsafe) and the policy claim (restriction is achievable). You have evidence for the first but not the second. If restriction is not feasible, then "don't use it" is not a prescription. It is an aspiration. And the policy question matters because the people in this room are not deciding whether AI should exist. They are deciding what to do given that it does exist. Your evidence addresses a world that might be desirable but does not exist.

**Maya**: Vic is right on the policy point, but I want to push on something else. Reva, you say you are a scientist evaluating a safety claim. But you are not evaluating it — you are rejecting the methodology before examining it. The cyberneutics framework has inspectable reasoning records, calibration registers, adversarial committee structures. You have not engaged with any of these mechanisms. You have dismissed the whole framework as "a methodology document and anecdotal experience." Have you read the methodology? Can you identify a specific mechanism that would fail under load?

**Reva**: I do not need to read the methodology to know that a safety claim without controlled evidence is insufficient. If a pharmaceutical company brought me a drug with a promising mechanism of action and no clinical trials, I would not evaluate the mechanism. I would tell them to run the trial. The mechanism may be brilliant. It may work. But "promising mechanism" is not evidence of safety or efficacy. This is basic scientific epistemology.

**Joe**: The pharmaceutical analogy is instructive but imperfect. Drugs are single interventions with controlled dosing administered by licensed professionals under regulatory oversight. AI use is a behavioral practice adopted voluntarily by millions of people with no regulation, no licensing, and no enforcement. The regulatory apparatus you are implicitly assuming does not exist and will not exist in the relevant timeframe. The question is what to do in the meantime. When we lacked FDA regulation of food safety, the answer was not "don't eat." It was "here is what we know about safe preparation, use it while we build the regulatory infrastructure."

**Reva**: "Here is what we know about safe preparation" — and what do we know? Where is the evidence that structured AI use reduces psychosis risk? You are telling people it is safe to eat at this restaurant because the chef seems careful, without a health inspection, without food safety training, without knowing what is in the kitchen. The confidence is not warranted by the evidence.

## Round 2: The Denominator and the Duty

**Frankie**: Reva, I want to engage with your strongest argument — the denominator problem. You say the framework reaches only voluntary adopters and the population at risk is the population least likely to adopt it. I concede that point. It is correct. But it proves less than you think.

Every safety innovation in history has this property. Seatbelts were adopted voluntarily by safety-conscious drivers for decades before they became mandatory. During that period, the reckless drivers — the population most at risk — did not wear them. By your logic, developing seatbelts was irresponsible because the safety-conscious drivers were already safer than average, and developing seatbelts "normalized" driving for the reckless ones. That is an absurd conclusion, but it follows from your argument structure. The sequence is: develop the safety practice, demonstrate its value, build the evidence base, then push for broader adoption. You are demanding the last step before allowing the first.

**Reva**: Seatbelts are a passive safety device that requires no skill to use and imposes no behavioral discipline. You are comparing them to a framework that requires sustained cognitive effort, inspectable record-keeping, adversarial evaluation, and calibration against outcomes. Those are not comparable adoption curves. A better analogy is asking drivers to complete defensive driving courses. We have forty years of data showing that voluntary defensive driving education has negligible population-level impact on traffic fatalities. The drivers who take the courses are already the safe ones. Your framework has the same adoption problem, and you are using the seatbelt analogy to hide it.

**Tammy**: I want to formalize what Frankie and Reva are actually disagreeing about, because it is not what it looks like. The disagreement is not about whether the framework helps its direct users. Assume it does. The disagreement is about the sign of the externality. Frankie says the externality is positive: developing structured-use practice creates knowledge, tools, and eventually cultural norms that propagate outward, improving safety for the broader population over time. Reva says the externality is negative: developing structured-use practice normalizes AI use, which expands the user base, which increases the number of unstructured encounters and thus the number of harms.

Both externalities are real. The question is empirical: which one dominates? And over what time horizon? If we are optimizing for next quarter, Reva is probably right — more engagement means more unstructured encounters means more harm, and the safety benefits have not propagated yet. If we are optimizing for five years, the answer depends on whether structured-use practice generates institutional infrastructure — norms, training, policy advocacy — or remains a boutique practice for careful individuals. The systems question is about propagation rate.

**Maya**: I want to challenge the optimistic side of Tammy's framing. "Develops into institutional infrastructure" assumes the practitioners who build the safety culture have political influence. Who actually shapes AI policy? Not careful practitioners with inspectable reasoning records. The companies shaping policy are OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta — entities spending hundreds of millions on lobbying and regulatory capture. The idea that a small-scale structured-use practice will propagate into meaningful institutional change is a fantasy unless we can identify the mechanism. How does "practitioner builds good habits" translate into "regulation protects vulnerable users"? I want to see the causal chain, not the aspiration.

**Reva**: Maya names the problem precisely. The causal chain from "informed practitioner uses AI carefully" to "vulnerable teenager is protected from chatbot-induced psychosis" does not exist. There is no mechanism. There is no propagation pathway. There is hope, and there is the flattering belief that one's own careful practice contributes to a better world. I have seen that belief before. It is how recycling became a substitute for regulating industrial pollution — individual virtue as a replacement for structural change. The framework is recycling. The problem requires regulation.

**Frankie**: I reject the recycling analogy, and I will say why. Recycling is a displacement activity — it makes people feel good without addressing the structural problem. The cyberneutics framework is not a displacement activity. It produces artifacts: inspectable reasoning records, calibration data, documented failure modes. These artifacts are the raw material for the regulatory infrastructure you are both demanding. You cannot regulate structured AI use without defining what structured AI use looks like. You cannot write standards without exemplars. You cannot build training programs without a methodology to teach. The framework is not a substitute for regulation. It is a prerequisite for regulation. Demanding regulation first is demanding the roof before the foundation.

- **New tension**: Tammy's time-horizon framing clarifies that Reva and Frankie may both be right — over different timeframes. The committee needs to adjudicate the time horizon.
- **Maya's challenge to propagation** is unresolved: the mechanism from practitioner practice to institutional protection has not been specified.
- **Reva's recycling analogy** is sharp but contested: the question is whether the framework produces artifacts with regulatory utility or just individual virtue.
- **Status**: Reva's denominator argument is partially addressed (Frankie's "prerequisite" counter) but the propagation mechanism is still vague. The self-sealing pattern has been surfaced but not fully confronted.

## Round 3: Failure Modes and the Counterfactual

**Vic**: I have listened to two rounds of debate about whether structured use is good for the world. I want to return to the engineering question Reva asked in her opening statement, which nobody has answered: what is the failure-mode analysis for the structured version?

I will propose failure modes and ask the proponents to respond. Failure mode one: the inspectable reasoning record creates an illusion of rigor. A practitioner generates a committee deliberation, reads the transcript, sees that multiple perspectives were explored, and concludes their decision is well-grounded — when in fact the deliberation was internally coherent but decoupled from reality. The audit trail looks clean. The reasoning looks complete. And the conclusion is wrong because the inputs were wrong or the question was misframed. This is the Enron audit problem: clean records that mask bad fundamentals.

**Tammy**: That is a real failure mode, and I want to characterize when it activates. It activates when the practitioner treats the deliberation output as a conclusion rather than an input — when the inspectable record becomes a justification rather than a checkpoint. The architectural defense is the review skill: an independent evaluation that treats the deliberation as a found document and scores it against rubrics. But Vic is right that the defense depends on the practitioner actually running the review and acting on it. If they don't, the inspectable record is exactly the false assurance Vic describes.

**Vic**: Failure mode two: calibration drift. The calibration register works by comparing predictions to outcomes. But calibration requires outcomes — which means waiting for reality to provide feedback. For decisions with long time horizons or diffuse effects, the feedback may never arrive in usable form. The practitioner calibrates well on short-cycle decisions and generalizes that calibration to long-cycle decisions where it does not apply. Overconfidence migrates from the calibrated domain to the uncalibrated domain.

**Joe**: I recognize this pattern from institutional risk management. Financial firms calibrated their risk models on ten years of data from a stable market and then applied those models to a structural shift they had never seen. The calibration was excellent within domain and catastrophically wrong out of domain. The structured-use framework has the same vulnerability: it calibrates on the decisions the practitioner makes regularly and provides no protection against the novel decision where all the usual patterns break down.

**Reva**: I note that Vic and Joe are now cataloguing the failure modes I said had not been characterized, and that the proponents are agreeing these are real vulnerabilities. The inspectable record can create false assurance. The calibration can drift out of domain. The review depends on practitioner discipline that cannot be enforced. These are not hypothetical — they are the structural analogues of the exact problems that caused the catastrophic cases in unstructured use. In unstructured use, the user trusts the AI because it sounds authoritative. In structured use, the user trusts the process because it looks rigorous. The epistemic error is the same: trusting form over substance.

**Vic**: I want to be fair to my own question. Those failure modes are real, and Reva is right to press them. But the relevant comparison is not "structured use with failure modes" versus "no use." It is "structured use with failure modes" versus "unstructured use with worse failure modes" versus "non-use with its own failure modes." Reva, what is the failure-mode analysis for non-use? You are a scientist making decisions under uncertainty. Without AI-mediated structured deliberation, what is your method for ensuring you are not falling prey to confirmation bias, anchoring, or the self-sealing reasoning pattern that this committee has identified in your own position?

**Reva**: I use peer review, replication, statistical methodology, and the norms of scientific inquiry that have produced reliable knowledge for centuries without generative AI. The suggestion that I need an AI to think clearly is precisely the techno-solutionism I object to. Human cognitive tools — structured argument, adversarial peer review, empirical testing — predate AI by millennia and have a characterized track record. AI does not.

**Vic**: Peer review has a well-documented replication crisis. Statistical methodology is plagued by p-hacking and publication bias. Scientific norms failed to prevent decades of fraudulent research in multiple fields. These are not AI-specific failures — they are failures of the human cognitive tools you are holding up as the comparison standard. I am not saying AI is better. I am saying the counterfactual is not "reliable human reasoning" — it is "human reasoning with its own well-documented failure modes." The honest comparison is imperfect tools versus imperfect tools, not imperfect tools versus reliable alternatives.

**Reva**: The replication crisis is real, but it was discovered through the scientific method itself — the tools that produced the problem also diagnosed it. That is the difference. The scientific method has a self-correction mechanism: empirical testing against reality. What is the self-correction mechanism for an AI-mediated deliberation? If the committee produces a plausible-sounding but wrong conclusion, what catches it? Another AI-mediated evaluation? You are proposing a system that validates itself.

**Tammy**: This is the deepest tension in the deliberation and I want to name it precisely. Reva is describing a hermeneutic circle: the AI generates a deliberation, the AI evaluates the deliberation, the AI remediates based on the evaluation. The same system is generator and evaluator. Even with rubrics and scoring and adversarial review, it is still the same model's judgments all the way down. The human is the circuit-breaker. The entire architecture depends on a human who reads the output critically, compares it to reality, and overrides when the internal coherence is decoupled from external truth. If the human is not doing that — if they are using the framework as a decision machine rather than a thinking aid — the hermeneutic circle closes and the framework fails silently.

### Round 3 Analysis

- **Emerging consensus**: The committee agrees on three characterized failure modes of structured use: (1) false assurance from clean-looking records, (2) calibration drift across domain boundaries, (3) hermeneutic closure when the human does not serve as circuit-breaker.
- **Critical insight from Tammy**: The entire architecture depends on the human remaining the active quality-control layer. The framework's value is as a thinking aid, not a decision machine. When this distinction collapses, the framework fails.
- **Reva scored a point**: Her observation that structured use can fail via the same epistemic error as unstructured use (trusting form over substance) is substantive and has not been refuted.
- **Vic scored a point**: The counterfactual is not reliable human reasoning but flawed human reasoning with its own documented failure modes. Non-use is not a safe default.
- **Status**: The failure modes have been characterized. The self-correction question (Reva's strongest remaining argument) is partially addressed by Tammy's human-as-circuit-breaker framing but the enforcement problem remains.
- **Next**: The committee must move toward synthesis. What framework recommendations survive the adversarial challenge?

## Round 4: Toward Conditions

**Frankie**: I want to propose a framework that takes Reva's objections seriously rather than dismissing them. Reva is right that the safety case for structured AI use has not been established by controlled study. She is right that the failure modes include false assurance and calibration drift. She is right that the framework reaches voluntary adopters, not the population most at risk. These are not minor concessions — they are the core of her argument.

What she has not established is that non-engagement is the superior response. And the committee has identified why: non-engagement does not reduce the number of AI users, does not build safety infrastructure, does not generate the regulatory raw materials, and does not address the informed practitioner's own cognitive limitations. So I propose the following framing: structured AI use is a *provisional* practice, explicitly conditional on evidence generation. The practitioner engages *in order to generate the evidence Reva demands* — not because the safety case is already established, but because the safety case cannot be established without engagement.

The conditions: (1) every structured-use session produces inspectable records that could serve as data for future evaluation; (2) the practitioner explicitly calibrates against outcomes, generating the longitudinal data Reva correctly notes is missing; (3) the framework documents its own failure modes as they are discovered, building the failure-mode analysis Reva demands; (4) the human remains the circuit-breaker — the framework is a thinking aid, not a decision machine, and this distinction is enforced through architectural design, not individual discipline.

**Reva**: I note that Frankie's proposal concedes my central point: the safety case has not been established. The rest of the proposal is "use it anyway, carefully, and generate evidence as you go." This is the same argument the tobacco industry made about filtered cigarettes: the harm is real, but if we engineer a mitigation and collect data as we go, continued use is justified. The data collection *is the continued exposure*. You are asking people to be experimental subjects in an uncontrolled trial of a technology with documented catastrophic failure modes.

**Joe**: Reva, the tobacco analogy fails on a specific structural point: the tobacco industry's data collection was designed to delay regulation, not to inform it. The data was proprietary, the methodology was hidden, and the purpose was to maintain sales. Frankie is proposing open methodology, inspectable records, and explicit failure-mode documentation. The incentive structure is opposite: the practitioner benefits from honest data because the practitioner is the one making decisions based on it. There is no engagement-metric incentive driving the data collection. You are applying the corporate-capture framework to a non-corporate practice.

**Maya**: Joe is right about the incentive difference, but I want to add a condition Frankie missed: the framework must include explicit off-ramps. If calibration data shows the framework is not producing better decisions than unstructured reasoning — if the longitudinal evidence goes against us — we stop. That commitment has to be real, not aspirational. Specify the metric, specify the threshold, specify what "stop" means in operational terms. Otherwise Reva is right: the data collection becomes a justification for continued use, not a genuine test.

**Vic**: I support Maya's addition. And I want to make explicit what Reva has accomplished in this deliberation, because it matters. Reva forced the committee to characterize three failure modes that were previously uncharacterized. She forced the committee to acknowledge that the safety case is provisional, not established. She forced the proposal to include evidence-generation conditions and off-ramps. These are not rhetorical victories — they are substantive improvements to the framework. The deliberation would have been weaker without her.

I also note that Reva's position has not been refuted on its own terms. Her demand for controlled evidence is the correct scientific standard. What the committee has established is that the correct scientific standard cannot be met without engagement, and that non-engagement has its own uncharacterized costs. The honest position is not "Reva is wrong" but "Reva is right about the evidence gap, and we have a characterized plan for closing it."

**Tammy**: I want to add one systemic condition to Frankie's framework. The human-as-circuit-breaker dependency is the single point of failure. The framework needs a mechanism for detecting when the circuit-breaker has disengaged — when the practitioner is rubber-stamping rather than critically evaluating. The calibration register can serve this function if it tracks not just prediction accuracy but metacognitive markers: how often does the practitioner override the committee's recommendation? How often does calibration data change their practice? A practitioner who never overrides and never changes practice in response to calibration data is a practitioner whose circuit-breaker has disengaged. The framework should flag this.

**Reva**: I want to be clear about what I am and am not conceding. The committee has produced a set of conditions under which structured AI use would be provisionally acceptable. Those conditions include: explicit evidence generation, characterized failure modes, off-ramps if the evidence is negative, architectural enforcement of human-as-circuit-breaker, and metacognitive monitoring for circuit-breaker disengagement. These are serious conditions. If they were met — genuinely met, not aspirationally described — I would update. My position is not "AI engagement is inherently evil." It is "the evidence does not currently support the safety claim." The conditions this committee has described would, if implemented and monitored, begin to generate the evidence I have been demanding.

What I will not concede is that describing these conditions is the same as implementing them. The gap between "we have a plan" and "we have a result" is where every engineering disaster lives. I will be watching for the plan to become a substitute for the result.

## Final Consensus

- **Unanimous**: The evidence base for harm from unstructured AI use is established and serious. No member of the committee disputes this.
- **Unanimous**: The safety case for structured AI use has NOT been established by controlled study. The claim is provisional.
- **Majority (5-1, Reva dissenting in part)**: Structured AI use by informed practitioners is provisionally justified as a method of generating the evidence needed to evaluate the safety claim, subject to explicit conditions.
- **Unanimous**: Three failure modes of structured use have been characterized: (1) false assurance from clean-looking records, (2) calibration drift across domain boundaries, (3) hermeneutic closure when the human ceases to function as circuit-breaker.
- **Unanimous**: The framework must include architectural enforcement of human-as-circuit-breaker, not merely aspirational guidelines.
- **Majority (4-2, Reva and Maya dissenting on sufficiency)**: The conditions described (evidence generation, failure-mode documentation, off-ramps, circuit-breaker monitoring) are sufficient for provisional adoption by informed practitioners.
- **Unanimous**: The precautionary position was substantively correct on the evidence and productive in forcing the committee to strengthen the framework. Reva's objections remain partially unresolved and must be tracked.

## KEY TENSIONS IDENTIFIED

1. **Evidence gap vs. evidence generation**: The safety case for structured use requires evidence that can only be generated through structured use. This circularity is real and unavoidable; the committee's response is to make the circularity explicit and conditional.
2. **Denominator problem**: The framework reaches voluntary adopters, not the at-risk population. This is conceded. The framework is not a public health intervention; it is a practitioner discipline. It does not address the 3 AM teenager problem and should not claim to.
3. **Hermeneutic closure**: The same model generates and evaluates deliberations. The human is the circuit-breaker. If the human disengages, the framework fails silently. This is the most dangerous failure mode.
4. **Time horizon disagreement**: Reva optimizes for immediate harm reduction; others optimize for long-term infrastructure building. Both are legitimate; the committee chose infrastructure with explicit conditions rather than withdrawal.
5. **Self-sealing reasoning**: Identified in both the precautionary position (refusal to engage forecloses testing the refusal) and the structured-use position (generating evidence for a practice you are already committed to). Both sides are vulnerable.

## ASSUMPTIONS SURFACED

- **The technology is permanent**: All committee members except Reva assume AI is here to stay. Reva's restriction scenario has no historical precedent supporting feasibility (Vic).
- **Informed practitioners are distinct from the general population**: The framework is designed for voluntary, disciplined adopters. It does not scale to mass adoption without additional institutional infrastructure.
- **Inspectable records have regulatory utility**: Frankie's "prerequisite for regulation" argument assumes future regulators will find these records useful. This is plausible but unverified.
- **Calibration generalizes across decision types**: The framework assumes short-cycle calibration informs long-cycle decisions. Joe and Vic identified this as a known failure mode.
- **The human will remain engaged**: The entire framework depends on a single point of failure — the human serving as active quality-control, not passive consumer. Tammy proposed monitoring for disengagement.

## EVIDENCE REQUIREMENTS

- A controlled comparison of structured vs. unstructured AI deliberation on decision quality, with pre-registration and independent evaluation (Reva's demand, accepted by the committee as a legitimate goal)
- Longitudinal calibration data: do practitioners who use the framework show improved calibration over time compared to practitioners who do not? (minimum 12 months, minimum 10 practitioners)
- Failure-mode inventory: systematic documentation of cases where the framework produced false assurance, calibration drift, or hermeneutic closure
- Off-ramp metrics: pre-specified criteria under which the practice would be suspended (to be defined before provisional adoption begins)
- Circuit-breaker monitoring: operational metric showing frequency of human override and practice change in response to calibration data

## DECISION SPACE MAP

This is not a simple yes/no. The decision space has four regions:

1. **Unrestricted use**: Use AI without structured framework. Current evidence says this produces documented harms at population scale. No committee member supports this.
2. **Structured provisional use with conditions**: Use AI within the cyberneutics framework, explicitly generating evidence to evaluate the safety claim, with characterized failure modes, off-ramps, and circuit-breaker monitoring. Supported by majority.
3. **Precautionary non-engagement**: Do not use AI for narrative/sensemaking tasks until controlled evidence of safety exists. Coherent position, but non-engagement also has costs (loss of safety-infrastructure development, ceding the field to unstructured users, not closing the evidence gap). Supported by Reva.
4. **Wait for regulation**: Defer to institutional regulators. No regulatory framework exists or is imminent. Not a viable near-term option.

If you optimize for **immediate harm minimization**: choose option 3. If you optimize for **long-term safety infrastructure**: choose option 2. If you optimize for **evidence generation**: choose option 2 with strict adherence to off-ramps.

## RECOMMENDED NEXT STEPS

1. **Define off-ramp metrics**: Before further provisional use, specify what evidence would trigger suspension. This is the concrete action that separates honest provisonal use from evidence-laundering for a predetermined conclusion.
2. **Begin longitudinal calibration tracking**: Minimum 12-month program, minimum 10 practitioners, comparing prediction accuracy over time. This generates the evidence Reva demands.
3. **Publish the failure-mode inventory**: The three failure modes characterized in this deliberation (false assurance, calibration drift, hermeneutic closure) should be documented in the framework materials, not buried in a deliberation transcript.
4. **Design the circuit-breaker monitor**: Implement Tammy's metacognitive tracking — override frequency, practice-change frequency, calibration-response rate — as an operational metric.
5. **Run the controlled comparison**: Design the study Reva asked for. Structured AI deliberation vs. structured non-AI deliberation on decision quality. Pre-register. Seek independent evaluation. This is the evidence that would genuinely resolve the core disagreement.
